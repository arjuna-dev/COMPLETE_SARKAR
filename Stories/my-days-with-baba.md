# PREFACE

Márga Guru Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti was a perennial source of spiritual power, a transcendental genius. His original thoughts on spiritual knowledge, science and philosophy, His striking theories on sociology and economics, and His penetrating insights into culture, art and literature, have already begun to stir the minds of thinking people all over the world. He has left an indelible mark through His numerous books. The songs of Prabhát Saḿgiita, numbering 5018, were written in an inimitable style. They have ushered in a new era in musical expression. These songs, written between 1982 and 1990, enchant in every way – in their rhythm, harmony, resonance and expression.

He established Ánanda Márga Pracáraka Saḿgha in 1955. Under His guidance and wisdom, it expanded into a global organization with branches in many countries around the world. He developed a dynamic spiritual practice that all people can do to gain inner peace and realization. Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti was not only a spiritual master, He was a *samája guru* (guide for society) as well and was concerned about the welfare of the society at large. He propounded Prout (the Progressive Utilization Theory), a socio-economic theory that offers solutions to the myriad of problems facing society today. He gave Neohumanism and the theory of microvita, whose explanatory powers will carry humanity to new scientific heights.

His teachings are invaluable. He has shown the way to spiritual enlightenment for all who desire a sublime life. His thoughts, words and deeds – and more – remained in perfect

harmony. Moreover, He trained His disciples to live in the same way. He was a living example of the practical lessons each one of us must learn to live a spiritually-balanced life.

These are some of the many reasons that thousands of people all over the world regard Him not just as a teacher, but as their very own – their most revered and affectionate father, their “Bábá”.

This book is a humble venture to throw light on some of the sublime characteristics of my *gurudeva* (master). I witnessed many of the incidents and anecdotes narrated in this book when I was fortunate enough to enjoy close proximity to Him. Yet my experience was no more than a small part of His drama. It is hard – no, impossible – for an ordinary man like me to fathom the depths of His personality.

Let me say something of the background to many of these stories. Spurred on by political interests, some unscrupulous politicians and the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) hatched a conspiracy against Bábá. They arrested Him in Patna, Bihar on 29 December 1971. He bore seven years of unjust imprisonment, during which harassments and inhuman torture was inflicted on Him. His health broke down as He suffered at the hands of a heartless and antagonistic alliance of politicians, CBI and jail authorities. But He never compromised His exalted principles of life even in adverse circumstances. He embodied robust optimism even when enclosed within prison walls. As a fellow prisoner, I witnessed much of this hidden story and have aimed to share some of those moments in this little book.

Many of these stories may appear to be minor incidents, but I think they contain valuable lessons and will be of immense help to the reader. Each story is like a tiny window allowing us to get a peep at an immense, brilliant sun. We may not be able to see the whole sun in one view, but we will get a glimmering of its magnificence. Underlying every single word and action of Bábá’s was a multi-purpose objective. I have tried my best to highlight events that will depict His immeasurable personality and His commitment to a principled life.

The circumstances of jail life did not allow me to maintain a diary. So I depend on my memory to call forth those unusual days of misery and delight. As time passed, I began to be less sure that I could recall everything clearly if I delayed any longer in putting my experiences down in black and white. I have left no stone unturned in giving a faithful account of my association in jail with Bábá.

Let me admit that I have no literary talent to bring to the task of portraying Bábá’s greatness. But I remember Bábá once saying to me, “You must write everything about your experiences whenever you get an opportunity.” So however limited my capacity may be, I have penned this book in deference to His instructions of long ago.

Knowing my weakness of expression, I long hesitated to embark on what was for me such a mammoth task. I am indebted to our respected Ácárya Vijayánanda Avadhúta, who encouraged me to write and assisted me in planning and editing the book, despite his engagements and organizational responsibilities. I also wish to acknowledge the hard work of Shrii Biresh Bose, Krishnanagar and Prof. Anil Kumar Banerjee (Ret’d.) of Kalyani University for translating from the Bengali original; and to Avadhútiká Ánanda Rucirá Ácáryá who helped me retell the stories in English for this edition. I am grateful for their untiring efforts in the making of this book.

Last but not least, I shall feel that my labour has been amply rewarded if you the readers benefit from reading

about the high ideals and teachings of Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti.

Ácárya Sarveshvaránanda Avadhúta

CHAPTER 1

# CONDUCT IS THE VEHICLE OF IDEALS

Swami Vivekananda once wrote, “Conduct is the vehicle of ideals. If you really want to judge the character of a man, look not at his great performances.... Watch a man do his most common actions; those are indeed the things which will tell you the real character of a great man. Great occasions rouse even the lowest of human beings to some kind of greatness, but he alone is the really great man whose character is great always, the same wherever he be.” (*Karma Yoga* by Swami Vivekananda)

One should not ignore the daily activities and conduct of a man in writing his biography because if we want to know something of the true greatness of someone, we should look into his or her daily life. Some biographies focus only on the person’s teachings and glories, but neglect the essential character of the person, and leave the reader unimpressed and unaffected. Although this is not a biography in the strict sense, I wish to convey a little of some aspects of Bábá’s personality. The saintly Chaitanya Mahaprabhu once said, “Practise before you preach.” A person whose life is worth emulating is one who has made every moment extraordinary by his or her exemplary conduct. Such a one will be acclaimed by the people and his or her name will be written in gold in the annals of society. When we look at the life of Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti we find His daily life was so sublime that everyone found in Him inspiration and guidance.

### Advance Preparations

On 29 December 1971, the CBI arrested Bábá. For His devotees it came as a total surprise. But for Bábá it was not unexpected. A few days before His arrest Bábá asked for me to come into His room. Along with me came His Personal Assistant (PA). Gravely He told us what to expect in the coming days.

“Look,” He said, “I would like to say a few things to you. It is urgent that I tell it now. A deep conspiracy has been hatched against Ananda Marga. As the founder, I am the principal target of that attack. At any moment I will be arrested by them. They will go to any length in misusing their power, but don’t be afraid of this. This situation is the outcome of a grim struggle of ideals. Righteousness (dharma) is sure to win in the end.”

He added, “Take care of this case. It contains some of my personal necessities and clothing. Don’t forget to send this case with me when the time comes.”

Then Bábá turned to me. “Though there is no sense in telling you about the case,” He said affectionately, “for they are not going to spare you this time either.”

This warning from Bábá gave us a shock. The information that there was a conspiracy going on had been coming to our ears for some time. Still we could never believe that our beloved Bábá would be put into prison! Any doubts we had were now removed, and it was clear that Bábá was making the necessary arrangements. Through Bábá’s brief warning, we realized that the coming ordeal was going to be long and drawn out. From then on we watched the dramas unfold according to Bábá’s unerring predictions.

Through its long history, our organization had to face innumerable obstacles and opposition. These obstacles had to be carefully countered with discretion. Advance preparations sometimes had to be made. Bábá would forewarn us about what was going to happen and prepare us with necessary instructions. Unbelievable as it seems, it is true. The mission was His child, He guided it with steady steps towards its bright future.

Every act of the mission bore the stamp of Bábá’s omniscience. He knew when there would be arrests, and that they would be followed by long imprisonment. He foresaw a period of terrible trial and tribulation for His followers. He gave the Sixteen Points just before His arrest perhaps because He knew of the ordeals to come and that the points would galvanize and inspire His devotees for the mission’s work in His physical absence. Bábá’s long imprisonment and an organized anti-Marga campaign naturally lowered the morale of the devotees. The Sixteen Points acted as an impenetrable shield and kept the devotees active and strong.

### The Jail Years

December 29, 1971, is a memorable day in the history of Ananda Marga. On that day the notorious CBI arrested Marga Guru Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti at His residence in Pataliputra Colony, Patna, and me from our organization’s Central Office next to it. Barun Kumar Mukherji and Pavitra Kumar Roy, both from Howrah, West Bengal, were also arrested at different places. Bábá was sent to Buxar jail immediately. We others were sent to Bankipur Central Jail, Patna. Some unfounded charges were eventually cooked up against us and we had to stand trial in the Patna court. We faced a mockery of trials in one court after another. We remained behind bars for seven long years until we were

finally vindicated.

Immediately after His arrest, Gurudeva was imprisoned in Buxar jail in Bihar for two months, and then on 3 April 1972, He was transferred to Bankipur Central Jail where we had been incarcerated from the start. In all this there was, for me at least, one advantage. This conspiracy allowed me to live with Bábá in close proximity. It was a godsend because not only did I enjoy His sweet closeness but also saw how His lifestyle conformed to His teachings.

During those years in Bankipur jail, Bábá was usually in the company of several of His disciples and wholetime workers. According to the progress of our various cases and court hearings the number varied from one to six persons. In Patna jail, we usually lived in three dank cells. As an under-trial prisoner, Bábá was categorized with a "Class One" rating and had with certain liberties not accorded to general prisoners. Although we were also arrested for the same reasons, we were not given that rating until much later. Barun Kumar usually served Bábá's food and Pavitra Kumar looked after things generally. We looked after Him collectively, taking turns cleaning, washing and doing our best to see that He kept His health up. The false charges, harassments and tortures we endured were mitigated by the blessing of living with and caring for our beloved master.

### Strict Adherence to Sixteen Points

Between 1955, the year Ananda Marga was founded, and 1971, the year of His arrest, He had given hundreds of discourses. What impressed me most was His determination to follow and practise those teachings that He gave for others to follow. His thoughts, words and actions were in perfect harmony, and the clarity of His pleasing personality made me think that His own lifestyle was the most appropriate example for His eternal message of universality.

One time Bábá said, "It is your conduct which speaks of your ideals. Your scholarship, your social or economic status in no way reflect your adherence to ideals." So everyone, whether they are householders, renunciates, wholetime or part-time missionaries, must live by certain conduct and social rules. We should remember that it is our conduct that sets us apart from other created beings. Bábá prescribed certain social norms for everyone so that people might elevate their daily existence to a divine life. And He followed those norms scrupulously Himself. Some of those prescriptions were set out in a series of disciplines called the Sixteen Points. A few stories will illustrate what I mean.

In jail, Bábá repeatedly discussed with us the far-reaching benefits of practising the Sixteen Points. These sixteen disciplines are aids for a *sádhaka* (spiritual aspirant). They help to purify the body and mind in preparing oneself for *sádhaná* (spiritual practices). They are: (1) wash oneself with water after urination, (2) men should be circumcised or keep the foreskin of the male organ pulled back, (3) keep joint hair clean and oiled, (4) use tight-fitting underwear (*lauṋgot́a*), (5) *vyápak shaoca*, or "half bath", (6) regular and systematic bath, (7) proper (vegetarian) food, (8) regular fasting, (9) follow strictly one's spiritual practices, (10) be uncompromising regarding one's Iśt́a (personal Goal), (11) be uncompromising regarding one's ideology, (12) follow the Supreme Command, (13) follow strictly one's rules of conduct, (14) always remember and follow one's oaths, (15) join in weekly *dharmacakra* (collective meditation), and (16) fulfil one's social duties. These points ensure physical purity and well-being and rid the mind of wanton influences. They help people prosper in their spiritual life

and inspire them to *Átmámokśárthaḿ jagat hitáya ca* (“For self-realization and the welfare of the universe”).

The Sixteen Points confer immense benefits in both individual and collective life. Bábá kept a strict watch over His disciples that they should follow them unwaveringly. As He was adamant Himself, He would show anger with any deviation on our part.

*Vyápak shaoca*\* is one of the points. It should be done before doing spiritual practices, eating and going to bed. One should wash the face by splashing one’s open eyes twelve times with the mouth simultaneously full of water. As the instructions are to splash the eyes twelve times, most of us do so twelve times and not more. Gurudeva did *vyápak shaoca* regularly. On chilly nights, He did *vyápak shaoca* using cold water. He kept this habit strictly. One night before bed, I watched Him splashing His eyes. I counted fifteen or sixteen splashes.

“Curious,” I thought. I said aloud to Him, “I thought according to the instructions, one has to splash the eyes twelve times. Why have you done three or four times more?”

“Well, you see,” Bábá said, with a smile on His lips, “I instructed that it should be done at least twelve times, but what’s the harm in doing more? It should not be fewer than twelve times. Besides, everything should be a few degrees beyond the prescribed code because if for some unavoidable reason one slips a little, he or she will never go beneath the minimum degree.”

---

\* *Vyápak shaoca* (pervasive cleanliness), also called “half-bath”, is to pour water over the urinary organ, arms and hands, legs and feet, and slash it on the face. It cools the senses and organs, and helps the body function more efficiently.

### Fasting Is a Sacred Vow

It was May or June 1972. Bábá was in a small cell of Bankipur jail. The place was thoroughly unhygienic, unfit for human habitation. There was no window or ventilator in the cell and the blistering heat of summer turned the cell into a veritable hell. It was impossible to get used to the suffocating heat and we used to perspire floods.

What we were afraid of came about at last. The unhygienic conditions began to tell upon Bábá’s health. Although He was constantly drenched in sweat, He was always in a restful mood. We appealed to the authorities to install an air cooler or at least a fan. Though the courts had authorized our request, the jail authorities turned down our appeal and were reluctant to even allow us to install a fan at our own cost. Thus circumstances forced Him to remain in that tiny cell; He braved the sweltering heat through mental force alone. As the days passed, His condition turned from bad to worse; His ability to digest food diminished and He lost His appetite.

We were at our wit’s end as to what to do. His health was breaking down and we thought that the worst might happen at any moment. So we cajoled Him into taking some food. He took almost nothing at night in that summer heat. If we urged Him a lot, He would say, “I have lost my appetite and I will be better if I don’t take anything at night.”

All our persuasion was in vain. He often went to bed without food. If the next day was a fasting day,\* I insisted

---

\* Fasting days are fixed. Two days in a month, called *ekádashiis*, are prescribed for family people and four days (the *ekádashiis*, plus the new and full moon days) are prescribed for monks and nuns. One should fast from dawn to dawn without water.

that He take something at night, but He turned a deaf ear and would not even drink liquids, let alone eat any solid food.

At dawn fasting started. We agonized over His ordeal of a twenty-four-hour fast without water in the furnace of that cell. What made it worse was that His diabetes was aggravated as a result of imprisonment. You may know that a diabetes patient must urinate frequently and that that results in dehydration. So, according to physicians, diabetes patients must drink water to forestall dehydration.

Bábá used to take a tumbler of water long before sunrise on the fasting day. The ruthless sun traversed the sky, pouring down its scorching rays, but on no account would He drink water though we brought tumblers of water before Him. Every time we requested Him to sip a little He would say, “I’m not thirsty yet.”

Once I continued to urge Him, “It is long past noon and surely you must be feeling out of sorts, particularly when your health is in such deplorable condition. Why not take just a little water?”

With a smile on His lips, He replied, “Whether I feel uncomfortable or not doesn’t matter. Fasting is a sacred vow, the observance of which must entail a little trouble. And it will help one achieve good results.”

Barun Kumar humbly reminded Him, “But the doctor has forbidden you to fast without water.”

Bábá firmly said, “Let the doctor say so. Fasting is a vow, isn’t it? I shall, of course, follow the instruction of the doctor, but only when I’m in dire need of water. I’ll drink when my physical condition demands it.”

We had nothing more to say more, yet we did not give up. Again we tried. “Your throat seems to have dried up, and your endurance has a limit.”

He replied promptly, “My throat is parched, no doubt, nevertheless thirst has not made inroads into my bosom yet. When it does, I’ll ask for water.”

Certainly His throat was parched, but never did He ask for water. When it was time to go to bed, we would silently stand in front of Him with a tumbler of water. Then He would say, “Well, you may give it now since it seems that my body needs it.” We knew that He had been thirsty since early in the day, but as He considered fasting as a sacred vow for a spiritual aspirant, He observed it strictly. He couldn’t bear being lax about the dos and don’ts of spiritual practice. After taking a sip, He handed the tumbler back.

“Why not drink all of it as you haven’t drunk all day?” I asked.

He said, “The water is meant to moisten the throat, not to fill the belly.” We tried to coax Him by various means to drink a little more, but all our arguments fell flat.

It made me restless to think of Bábá’s unrelenting attitude. Think of it! How He kept His vows and maintained His principles! Compare it to how tamely people compromise their ideals in the face of mild difficulties.

The mercury began to soar when Bábá was transferred to the Bankipur jail. As mentioned earlier, His health deteriorated not only for the unhygienic condition in the cell but also for the fiendish harassments perpetrated by the CBI and its associates. Very soon He was suffering from chronic dysentery, diarrhoea, bleeding piles and other diseases. He had to take medicine to combat these diseases, but His sufferings knew no end on the fasting days. He needed to urinate frequently, yet He would not take those medicines on fasting days because, He said, His vow would be broken, as the tablets and capsules had to be swallowed with water.

When we told Him that fasting on new-moon or full-moon days was not binding on Him (being a householder), He

brushed aside our argument by saying, “Though the rule applies to monks, how can I forget that I am the author of this rule? Besides why shouldn’t I fast four times a month if it is more beneficial for me?” We never succeeded in making Him take medicines on fasting days. All our arguments fell flat in front of His unflinching adherence to principle.

Let it be known that our fasting, too, during the summer in that jail, was truly unbearable. Somehow the days passed, but the nights were endless. We looked forward to the moment when the eastern horizon would be tinged with red.

Fasting without water in the fiery heat made us feel as if we were hapless travellers in a desert trapped in a mirage. No sooner did we see a streak of light above the horizon than we heaved a sigh of relief. We drank our water and felt like moving fossils come back to life. The door of the cell had been opened in the meantime, and we rushed to Bábá with a tumbler of water.

Before drinking water, He asked, “You have come, but has the sun risen?”

I said, “It is dawn, so you may drink water.”

Bábá said, “Go outside and confirm whether the sun has risen or not. I'll drink water only if the sun has risen.” Saying this, He put the tumbler on a nearby table. It was only when we confirmed that the sun was up that He drank the water.

### Sincerity in Spiritual Practice

After making arrangements for His bath and laying down a blanket for His spiritual practices, we used to return to our own cells, and Bábá would hang a curtain over the door. He did everything from bath to spiritual practices in that tiny cell.

On the mornings after fasting, before we left His cell, we said to Him, “You have been without food since the night before last, so please do your *sádhaná* quickly so that you may eat breakfast before it gets too late.”

He pulled the curtain down, smiled and agreed, “Let me see to it.”

We learned that it was only to please us that He replied in that manner. What we saw was that on those mornings He would do *sádhaná* for a longer time than on other days. Bringing in His breakfast, I said, “Why did you do *sádhaná* for so long?”

“Don't you know that mind easily gets concentrated on the mornings after fasting?” Bábá said, “So how can I cut my *sádhaná* short?” Bábá was so uncompromising about *sádhaná*. He never tolerated any laxity in this respect, whether He was well or ill, at home or on tour.

Let me relate something more. It was His usual practice to do evening *sádhaná* from five or six o'clock until eight or half-past eight. We followed the same schedule in a neighbouring cell. After *sádhaná* He would walk up and down in the open space in front of His cell until about nine. We would walk with Him. One night when we were walking with Him, I noticed that His lungi was quite soaked. I said, “Your lungi seems to be wet.”

He said bluntly, “Yes, it is wet, and it gets wet like this every day. As I do sádhaná for long hours in a closed cell, I perspire a lot and the upper part of the lungi gets soaked. So, after I sit up, I turn the lungi upside down so that it can dry during my evening stroll.”

There was no window in that tiny cell; there was not even a vent. No breeze could get access to it. Nevertheless, He remained absorbed in *sádhaná* despite the heat that caused Him to sweat profusely. Once He drew our attention to the

fact that the blanket on which he sat for *sádhaná* was wet all the way through, but He said in an unperturbed voice, “It is of no importance because it happens daily. The blanket gets wet when I do *sádhaná* for hours at a stretch.” He never shortened His *sádhaná* even a little bit though He must have suffocated in that closed cell. We never heard Him comment that the sweltering heat had in any way interfered with His *sádhaná*.

As Bábá had weak teeth, He was required to take special care of His teeth according to the instructions of His dentist. He was to use a special brand of toothpaste. But as He enjoined the monks to use an antiseptic cleaner like neem, an antiseptic herb, He stopped using the previous brand of toothpaste and began to use neem toothpaste. He wanted to show us that what was applicable to His workers was also binding upon Him. It was another example of His unwavering attitude, but in fact, it was a lesson that one can only impart the teachings that one practises. He was not ready to ever compromise on His principles, nor did He ever buckle under pressure.

### Respectful at All Times

Long ago in Ranchi, Bábá was going to the *jágrti* from our camp office. I was sitting with Him in the car.

As we were talking, Bábá inquired after a young worker, “When will he reach here?” I smiled a little and said what I could, but He asked, “Why are you grinning?”

He said, “Can you tell me why I have used such respectful terms for him?”

I bowed my head. “Just to teach me,”

“Yes,” He said. “It is in conformity with etiquette. It is advisable to use respectful language about an honourable man.”

The worker in question was very close to me, so during the conversation I had not used such language. Bábá taught me a practical lesson that day. It is hard, indeed impossible, for me to remember on how many hundreds of occasions He taught us about etiquette and the ins and outs of social conduct in this casual way.

How can I repay what I owe to Him? A line from a song of Prabhát Saḿgiita reverberates in my mind: “Never can this debt be repaid.”

Bábá was unrelenting in following His principles so far as the code of conduct was concerned. He neither compromised nor made even the slightest deviation from the prescribed rules. One may be curious to know why He was so strict.

Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti had attained perfection from the moment of His birth. He was the ultimate object of *sádhaná* for all people of all times. He taught His philosophy not only for the inspiration of society but to guide people step by step to perfection, unfolding their immense potentialities along the way. He made His life a resplendent example of how an ideal person should live and how one should inspire others. It explains why His own life was merged with the ideals He propagated. He was the only

# CHAPTER 2

## ALL-COMPASSIONATE BÁBÁ

As part of the rules that govern a worker's life, Bábá said that a worker should lend his or her ears to the sufferings of others and help them according to his or her might. Having renounced worldly life, one should not indulge in self-pity or go on recounting one's own weal and woe. A busy missionary does not have time to be concerned with his or her own petty sorrows. Nobility lies in removing the miseries of others and showing them that it is the sunshine, not the clouds, that is the *summum bonum* of life.

It was Bábá's way to follow Himself the rules He asked others to follow. The extreme circumstances of jail did not deter Him in this regard. In jail I had a chance to see Bábá's personality and equanimity stand strong and unwavering in the face of formidable circumstances. Throughout the stay in that dismal place, Bábá never talked about His personal circumstances, yet He was continually thinking of and helping alleviate the pains and misfortunes of others.

### Indifferent to His Own Ordeal

During His two months' stay in the Buxar jail He was not spared by the CBI officials who visited the jail on this or that pretext and badgered Him in a myriad of ways. When He fell ill owing to the inadequate diet and unsanitary conditions of the jail, He was shifted to the Patna Medical College for treatment. Then when He was transferred to the Bankipur Central jail on the orders of the Patna High Court, the responsibility of looking after Him devolved on those of us who had been arrested with Him months before.

It was ten o'clock on the first night at Bankipur. Bábá had just sat down to eat. The four of us served His food. Throughout His imprisonment, Bábá never took any food from the jail kitchen. His personal assistant used to bring food twice a day from His residence at Pataliputra Colony. The food would reach the jail at about five o'clock in the afternoon, and each and every item would be cold, stiff and almost inedible by the time He was ready for dinner. It perturbed us to think that Bábá would be having difficulty eating that food, but He never said anything about its quality. On that night He was talking as usual during our dinner on different topics. After having eaten only two *lucis* (fried flat bread), He stood up with a gesture of great satisfaction. He sat on His bed, and we shared out the remainder of the food from the food carrier. The *lucis* scarcely gone into our mouths when we exchanged strange looks. The message was clear. The *lucis* were too hard for any of us to chew; how much more difficult it must have been for Bábá, because His teeth were fragile.

Afterwards we sat beside Him and said, "The *lucis* were too hard to chew. How did you manage it? You must have found it inedible. Why didn't you say anything."

Bábá smiled and said, "It matters little if there is some small difficulty in eating the food. Those who cooked it with all their hearts and brought it from a distant place would certainly be unhappy if I said that I had trouble eating it or didn't savour it."

Goaded by curiosity I asked, "Did the same thing happen when you were in Buxar jail?"

Bábá said, "It was winter when I stayed there, so the food was even more stiff."

I said, "In the beginning of your stay there our workers were allowed to meet with you. If you had just once told them of the toughness of the food, they could have solved the problem by coating the *lucis* with ghee, clarified butter. They would have then been soft and you could have taken them with less difficulty."

Bábá said affectionately, "Why should I? For My sake they traveled a long distance and, full of devotion, carried the food carefully. So if I had made any adverse comment, their agony would have had no end."

It became clear to us that in no way would He tell others about His troubles however great His discomfort. He concealed His own sufferings from others lest they be pained. He was careful to see that others remained in high spirits even at the expense of His own comfort.

### Fried Brinjal in Buxar Jail

According to the jail regulations, upper class prisoners are given a "Class One" rating, and are assigned to them one or two other convicted prisoners to act as assistants. As Bábá belonged in this category, the Buxar jail authorities provided Him with two assistants, Ramchand and Kanhaia by name. Bábá told us about them. They were very fond of Him. They felt for His difficulty in eating the cold food that was brought to the jail. One day they decided they would themselves cook food for Bábá and serve Him hot meals. Though they did not know anything of the art of cooking, they resolved to fry slices of brinjal (aubergine) for Him. They washed and cut the brinjal into thick round slices, then started cooking with great fanfare. After that they served Bábá with great devotion. The fledgling cooks were glad to see that Bábá ate all the food.

They asked Him, "Do you relish it?"

Bábá enthusiastically replied, "Very good, very good."

Bábá wryly commented on the abilities of those enthusiastic cooks, “Do you know how they fried the brinjal? The upper side was half-cooked, the middle spongy and the bottom completely burnt!”

Needless to say, the fried brinjal was not at all edible, but as it was spiced with love, He took it delightedly. Ramchand and Kanhaia were overwhelmed with joy when Bábá lavished praise on them.

This reminds me of a story recounted in the *Mahábhárata*. Lord Krśna paid a sudden visit to the humble home of the righteous minister, Vidura. Vidura was out at the time, and his wife, a devotee of Krśna, nervously wondered how to best take care of Him. She stretched out a mat for Him to sit on and thought about what to do. Her problem was compounded by the fact that on that day there was nothing in her kitchen to serve her renowned guest. She then remembered that there were some bananas at her disposal.

She brought them quickly, began peeling them. But by then her devotion had reached such dizzy heights that she unmindfully handed the skins, not the bananas, to Krśna. Lord Krśna smilingly ate the peels one after another, as if He had never relished such tasty food. Meanwhile Vidura returned. He was taken aback by his wife’s queer behaviour. Vidura’s reaction brought his wife down to earth and she, realizing her blunder, was embarrassed. Vidura turned to Krśna and earnestly offered Him some bananas. Krśna said, “I was contented as long as your wife was serving me with single-minded devotion. But now that her mind has again returned to the state of duality, I’m afraid I won’t find the bananas as delicious as the skins.” In this way Lord Krśna ended His banquet at Vidura’s house.

### Compassion for People

In jail His equanimity and sympathy were evident time and again. We were always on hand when He took His meals. He would speak on different matters in His own inimitably interesting way, thinking little about the quality of the food. He took each and every item, albeit in a small quantity, but He never made any comment on it. Eating was really an unimportant matter to Him.

Bábá was quite absent-minded about His meals. He managed to forget by afternoon what He had taken at noon, or if He had enjoyed it. If we inquired, He said, “How can I remember in the afternoon what occurred in the morning? Well, perhaps I can say something if you ask me when I eat.”

Once, after Bábá had finished His meal, we sat together and shared among ourselves the excess food left in His food carrier. In his haste, the cook had forgotten something. The curry was absolutely without salt and almost inedible. Afterwards we sat beside Bábá and remarked about the tasteless meal. “Well, nobody can be meticulously careful every time about each and every item. There may be a slight slip-up on occasions, quite naturally. It’s not wise to dwell upon it.”

Bábá embraced the maxim “plain living, high thinking” through living a simple, unsophisticated life ornamented with lofty ideas. How could one whose entire being was directed towards the welfare and happiness of all take time to think of His own trivial matters? That is why the circumstances which would have otherwise affected Him never got a hold on His mind – a mind which was immanent and all-embracing.

### Mattress without Stuffing

As Bábá was opposed to using anything granted by the government, we had a mattress brought for Him from His residence. Though it was not very big, still it dangled over the sides of His narrow cot. At the onset of winter we decided to bring a mattress to fit the cot. We sent a message to that effect and a new mattress soon reached us. We removed the old mattress from the bed and were dismayed to find that the cotton matting in the middle had drifted towards the edges. The sides of the mattress were bulging, and the middle was nothing but the thin outer covering. To say the least, it could no longer be called a mattress.

“The matting inside has drifted to the sides,” I said, “the bed must be as cold and hard as a plank. Why didn’t you say something to us?”

“Why do you bother about it?” Bábá indifferently said. “True, I began to feel that the bed was somewhat hard. But as it was serving its purpose, I thought it unnecessary to say anything.”

It was not to our credit that we had cocooned ourselves with the false notion that He would come to us about any difficulties. When Bábá had been transferred to Bankipur jail, our joy knew no bounds at the thought that we were getting a rare opportunity to be near Him. We believed that Bábá too felt glad. He had told us, “How can I express my joy to find you around me? I feel totally relieved by having you near me at last. You are my guardians. You should do whatever you think right as regards my medicines, diet and all else. You should have no reason to ask me.” We lamented our lapse into neglect for which He had to suffer.

He had made it clear to us that He had no time to think of personal matters, and became annoyed if we sought His opinion about His meals, medicine or diet. He only concerned Himself about the welfare of oppressed humanity, plants and animals. The sacred responsibility of looking after His physical well-being was unquestionably ours. As a consequence to the mattress affair, we redoubled our attention to His welfare. Nevertheless I cannot say that we were perfectly flawless in discharging our duty.

### Bankipur Jail – A Veritable Hell

Patna was reeling under a sweltering heat wave. Bábá braved the unhygienic living conditions which prevailed in the jail, but His health entirely broke down nevertheless. The inside of the cell was little better than a furnace. The effects of the intolerable heat were inescapable. It seemed that the ruthless jail authorities enjoyed seeing Him suffer. In spite of His “Class One” rating, they did what they could to obstruct and harass Him at every step. They refused to comply with the court order to install an electric fan in the cell.

Although Bábá had “Class One” status from the beginning of His incarceration, the rest of us in early 1972 had not yet been recognized as such. Every evening at 6:00 we were locked up, but as we were allowed to serve Bábá His evening meal, we would be allowed out again at 8:30 to join Bábá. At lock up time at 9:00 two of us would stay with Bábá in His cell through the night.

All that we had on hand was a pair of palm-leaf fans. Though we tried our best to ease His discomfort by fanning Him constantly with those little fans, they were no match for the blistering heat. Bábá could not rest at all at nights. For brief moments drowsiness made His eyelids heavy, but suddenly He would sit up – all His clothes completely

soaked in perspiration. The intense heat would keep Him awake throughout the night. It was really heart-rending to see Him in this condition. But even an ordeal of this magnitude did not overwhelm Him. Always a true affectionate father to us, He would tell us to go to sleep ourselves. “Have you not gone to bed yet? How long will you suffer, too? Go and lie down now.”

We could never understand how Bábá, clearly suffering night after night, never complained or bothered about it. On the contrary, He was anxious about others who were suffering, about us and the whole world at large.

No less agonizing was the daytime. We did not know how to cope with the heat wave which raged all day long. But regardless of the fury of nature, Bábá continued to concern Himself with the welfare of the workers of Ananda Marga, and how the people of India and abroad were facing the trials and tribulations. He would ask about the AMURT workers who were to reach different disaster sites with relief materials. He would not be satisfied to hear that the workers had reached those sites, but would calm down only when we were able to give Him a detailed account of the relief work being done. The call of humanity in distress touched the core of Bábá’s heart and overrode all concern about His own person, nor did He let His own sufferings distract Him in any way.

The dreadful days of summer were passing, leaving Bábá more and more feeble and exhausted. Finally after moving from pillar to post, we obtained permission to install an electric fan at our own cost. The newly installed fan was more a consolation than a reality because power-cuts were so frequent that more often than not we had to resort to the hand-held fans. After we had lulled Bábá to sleep at night, we slept in the small hours, though we never allowed ourselves to go into deep sleep, because there was every chance of power failure at night.

Before going to bed, Bábá repeatedly said to us, “You needn’t remain awake after I sleep, I’ll wake you if necessary.” But He never did call us once we slept. Often He had to leave His bed in the night. He would go stealthily to the toilet and remove the commode’s lid noiselessly so as not to disturb us. He was careful not to put others to trouble for His own sake.

### A Heart Filled with Affection.

Dinner was usually between ten and eleven at night. Afterwards Bábá would dwell upon this or that topic for some time and then we would go to bed. Even then He continued to tell us so many interesting stories. So that He might sleep early, we repeatedly had to remind Him that to stay up till the dead of night was detrimental to His health.

When we saw that Bábá had slept, we went to bed ourselves, but not before putting two tumblers of water on the table by His cot. Often Bábá felt thirsty at night, those and He drank water from the tumblers within His reach.

One night His stories were so absorbing that we forgot to put water aside. At midnight Bábá woke up and reached out towards the table. Finding the tumblers empty, He got up gently and refilled the tumblers from the pitcher in the corner of the room. He could have awakened us, but did not.

Even though Bábá was careful not to disturb our sleep, we found our sleep broken by His gentle footsteps. We awoke out of our slumber and the qualms of conscience drove us to Him. I wanted to take the tumbler from Him, but He said, “Why did you get up? Do you think that I can’t do such a small thing myself?”

I however remained adamant. I took the tumbler from His hand and fetched the water. After putting a second tumbler of water on the table, we went to bed once again.

It was hard to sleep for the rest of the night as, we could not escape thoughts of self-censure. Bábá’s affection for us was so profound that He did not like to disturb us even in case of dire necessity. He would not deprive His sons of their soothing sleep or cause any kind of discomfort to them. The endless flow of fatherly affection made Him unmindful of His own illnesses.

### The Empty Food Carrier

Bábá’s lunch and dinner that were brought in daily were stopped at the jail gate for inspection by the jail doctor, the jail authorities and the CBI men. It was only after they had given their okay that the food carrier would reach Bábá. More often than not, the inspecting officers came late or were busy in gossip, which delayed their delivery. Sometimes Bábá’s lunch did not come until late afternoon. To add an extra touch of harassment, the CBI men wanted the empty carrier returned upon the delivery of the full one. The slightest delay handing over the empty carrier to the officers made them suspicious. They thought we were trying to manoeuvre in some way with the food carrier.

This trouble was at its worst if dinner reached late in the evenings. On the one hand it was time for Bábá’s *sádhaná*, and on the other hand, the time for our going into the lock-up was fast approaching. We had to do everything in a hurry. Mistakes were bound to happen, such as the time I made a blunder.

It was nine p.m. We were already locked in our cell. Bábá, as usual, talked to us for some time. As the time approached, we asked Bábá to prepare for dinner.

Bábá said, “I am not at all hungry, you see. I don’t have any appetite.”

Bewildered, we thought this was strange because in the past few days His health had been improving, and He had been eating regularly. We thought that Bábá might feel hungry if we waited a little longer. After an hour or so we requested Him once again to eat, and He again reiterated that He had no appetite. He told us to take our meals. We continued to urge Him to eat a little something at least. Bábá stuck to His decision and even firmly declined to drink anything.

We had no choice but to sit for our dinner. We opened the food carrier – the containers were empty! Shocked, I thought, “Has the food flown away?” But the mystery cleared up when we realized that in my haste I had returned the full food carrier to the jail officers and not the empty one. My heart sank like a stone.

We ate hurriedly. All through the meal I imagined the scene as it might have been if I had opened the empty food carrier in front of Bábá as He waited for His dinner. Helpless in our confinement in the cell, we were unable to prepare anything for Him. I prayed to Mother Earth to open up and swallow me immediately!

Shamefaced, we went to His bedside and sat with our heads hung low and our hearts lower than low. Bábá affectionately beckoned us closer and said, “Sit down. What’s the matter? Why are you all so glum?”

There was nothing to hide from Him. He listened to us, but did not give any importance to what had happened. He said lightly, “You needn’t worry about it. You had to do everything in a hurry, so some mistakes are bound to occur. Why do you dwell on it so seriously? Besides I have no

appetite, so take it easy." He then changed subjects and diverted us from our anxiety.

We vowed to be more careful henceforth from then on so that it would not happen again. He smilingly endured hardship to pull us out of our morass of self-condemnation.

In spite of my shame, I was overjoyed as I became aware once again of His boundless affection flooding over us. Never did He bother about Himself. He was thinking always about how He could make others happy. His heart overflowed with love and His joy knew no bounds at the sight of others' happiness. The thought of suffering humanity touched a tender chord in the depths of His heart. His affection rained down on all and to made all people's hearts aglow with a divine ecstasy. This is why we called Him "Father" – the compassionate Bábá.

# FOLLOWING SELF-DISCIPLINE

Discipline is the foundation of a well-knit society. The stronger the sense of discipline, the more solid the framework of the society. For that reason personal discipline is necessary if we aspire to build a human society based on high ideals.

Self-discipline is indispensable in the life of a religious master who takes upon his shoulders the task of guiding humanity towards perfection. His sacred pledges will fail if he lacks in a sense of discipline and a strong commitment to his principles. He needs to set the example of absolute faith and obedience if he desires to bring about a renaissance in social life and a metamorphosis of human society. Bábá was the epitome of self-discipline. He was meticulous in everything – from directing the growth of a worldwide organization to the simple details of everyday life. Let me give a glimpse of this aspect of His character in jail.

### Tidiness – An Everyday Habit

In addition to the electric lamp we kept an old kerosene lamp, decrepit and rusty, in Bábá's cell because power load-shedding was a regular feature of life in those days. We often had to make do with the dim light of the kerosene lamp, everything made mysterious by the hovering shadows on the walls. The heat generated by its flame only added to the already unbearable closed stuffiness of the cell; so we kept the flame dimmed as far as possible.

It was late night. Load-shedding as usual plunged us into darkness. Suddenly I became aware of Bábá rummaging

through His box to find something. I sat to hold the lantern up for Him, but Bábá made a gesture and said, “What’s the need of light for this? I can get whatever I need, even in the pitch-dark, since everything has been kept neatly.” I discovered anew that Bábá was meticulously neat about everything.

A corner of the cell was set apart to keep His things. His toiletries were all kept in a precise way. Occasionally when He went out of the cell, we tried to dust them and replace them in their previous order. He knew every time it happened though we took care to do our best. That our sense of perfection was flawed was silently pointed out by Him.

Though He had two pairs of glasses, He put them aside most of the time as they were not required in the cell. But He asked for His glasses whenever He had to read. The reading glasses were kept in His shirt pocket. He told us exactly where we should put our hands so that we might not have to grope about.

“I make it a point to always keep the cased spectacles in the right-hand pocket and never in the left one. I keep my handkerchief in the left-hand pocket. If I put the handkerchief with other things, they are liable to fall out when I try to pull it out,” He said.

Bábá kept a wristwatch under His pillow and in case He needed it, He pushed His hand under the pillow and brought it out within a second.

“How can you get hold of the watch so easily from among all things kept under the pillow?” I asked.

Bábá lifted the pillow. “Just see the space for my watch under the pillow: the second case of glasses is just beside it, and the papers and the journals which I have not finished reading are next to the glasses. I can easily get the things I need.”

All this might seem trivial, but it carries its own insignificance. His neatness reflected the care He had for the objects of this world. Regularity, punctuality and looking after every detail were an integral part of His lifestyle. He was disciplined in the true sense of the term. As He wanted to train our minds to be equally ordered in our habits, He would not hesitate to show His displeasure if He came across indiscipline or sloppy work.

One time, immediately after morning *sádhaná* around 8:30, Bábá got ready for His bath. We kept a clean set of clothes at hand so that He could dress conveniently. The used clothes were then sent to the jail laundry. The washermen returned them washed and ironed. The clothes were kept in a fixed order, and Bábá would use them accordingly. One morning as He was about to go for His bath, we were to make ready a particular set of clothes. As ill-luck would have it, we could not immediately find that set. We thought to get another set from Bábá’s box, but Bábá objected. “What makes you flout the order? Do everything you can to find that set.” Bábá waited. We made a frantic search. It was finally found lying among a heap of clothes in the laundry. Only after we found those clothes did Bábá start His bath.

### Strict Schedule

Bábá was so strict in following His schedule that He never deviated from it even in the face of adverse situations. Whether in jail or at home He performed His daily programme according to schedule. Prior to imprisonment He was in the habit of doing *sádhaná* first; then came bath and breakfast; and a walk in the open air accompanied by some disciples and workers followed. During imprisonment Bábá

followed the same routine. In jail, too, He performed *sádhaná*, took His bath and ate His breakfast according to His own schedule every morning. He never budged an inch from this routine.

Those “field walks”, as they were called, were a highly delightful time for those who accompanied Him. During a walk He would speak on so many different subjects and His endless knowledge of the universe engulfed us. Those Margis who had that opportunity know how very blessed they were to get that divine experience.

There was no open space in the jail for a walk, but that did not hinder Him much. There was a narrow space in His cell. After eating breakfast Bábá, with a newspaper in His hand, paced slowly up and down the cell with us walking beside Him. While skimming through the pages of the newspaper, He talked on different matters with us. Time passed in this way, but not for a single moment did He deviate from the daily routine.

### Follow the Traffic Rules

In 1976 the country was in the grip of the Emergency.* Anybody could be harassed at anytime. The ordinary citizens were on the tenterhooks all the time. The wrath of the government fell especially on Ananda Marga for reasons best known to itself. Followers of Ananda Marga preferred to lie low to avoid arrest without trial and other harassments.

The impact of the Emergency reached the Patna jail, too. Its effect on the prisoners was discernible. They became even more servile than usual lest they be subjected to inhuman tortures at their hands.

Be that as it may, Bábá kept His usual equanimity – upright and undaunted. He behaved with the jail authorities quite differently from the expected norm. Far from submitting to them, He refused to talk to them and would not allow them to come near Him. If they came into the cell for any reason, He lay with His face towards the wall. They left the cell humiliated, knowing that they were thoroughly out of favour with Him.

Bábá repeated a proverb to us, “ ‘Like god, like offering’ (‘tit for tat’), isn’t it? They deny me the minimum amenities and now they are eager to show me lip service. It is a sin even to cast a glance at them.”

Once about 8:30 after evening *sádhaná*, Bábá was taking a stroll along a narrow strip in front of the cell. I was walking with Him. Bábá was speaking on the activities of Ananda Marga. We were keeping to the left side of the path. A handful of top-brass jail officials turned the corner. The Inspector General of Prisons (IG), flanked by the superintendent, the jailer and other subordinates, were coming towards us. They were walking in such a way that they occupied the full width of the path.

We were used to seeing the IG come into the jail under the cover of night. Obviously it accommodated him to conduct these trouble-free inspections because he did not have to hear the grievances of the prisoners, who were by that time locked up for the night. What is more, presumably, the nocturnal seclusion gave him an opportunity to strike deals with the jail superintendent.

The officials, led by the IG, the most important man in the prison administration, were bearing down on us. I said to

Bábá's snubbing silenced me. He was not one to compromise on principles. It drove home the lesson that the moral force thus generated is one of the strongest of forces. It explains why Bábá neither bowed to anybody nor buckled under pressure. The word “compromise” was not to be found in His dictionary.

Bábá had a timetable for His daily activities – the schedule which He followed with as much precision as possible. He knew the jail code permits “Class One” prisoners like Himself to remain outside their cells until 9 p.m. So after a brief stroll He returned to His cell before the clock struck nine. The prison guards sought to push prisoners into their cells before nine o'clock because they would be able to relax that much sooner. They tried to force Bábá into His cell before nine, and with this end in view, walked about jangling their keys. But Bábá remained unmoved and only started for His cell a minute or two before nine. Gradually the prison guards got used to it.

Responsibility, discipline and punctuality marked all of Bábá's actions. Whether it was during His government service in Jamalpur, or in mission work, Bábá had an exceptional sense of duty. I think that it was through these extraordinary qualities that He was able to create a dynamic and successful worldwide mission.

CHAPTER 4

# MAXIMUM UTILIZATION

Prout is a unique socio-economic and political theory propounded by Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti. The third principle stipulates that everything in this universe – mundane, meta-physical and spiritual, whether of the individual or of the Cosmos – should be utilized to its fullest. Bábá made His life an example of this precept. He demonstrated that there were no exceptions. We should be careful in using all things, big or small, for we do not have the right to waste any resource of this universe.

### Proper Utilization of All Things

I do not know if I shall ever forget a small incident that occurred in 1968 when our office was at Ranchi. Seated in His room after field walk, Bábá asked me to draft a letter. Accordingly I took a sheet of foolscap paper, wrote on it and read it to Him. He caught sight of the new sheet of paper.

“Don't use a new foolscap for drafting a letter,” He said, “There are old proof copies, cyclostyled and typed sheets of paper galore in our office. One side of each is always white. This paper can be used for rough drafts. It may help your office save only one rupee a month, but is this unimportant for a voluntary organization – even a rupee counts.”

Surely it is very important. In fact, there was no shortage of scrap paper lying in heaps in our office. I agreed that to use that scrap paper would help our organization curb petty expenditures. More importantly, it is not desirable to misuse anything or allow it to go to waste. Everything in the universe, big or small, has value. It would be remiss to waste or improperly use a thing only because it is small or trifling.

Bábá has written in *Caryácarya Part 2*, “Do not allow any wealth of the world to go to waste; take especially active steps to prevent the waste of food, fuel and water.” His clear directive and advice is that we should utilize the wealth of the world carefully, without waste and with the welfare of all living beings in mind.

### Waste Not...

Another example of this habit came to our notice one day when, around noon, we were cleaning the cell. Barun Kumar's eyes fell on the soap in the soap case. He called me and pointed at a small fragment of soap that had been pasted on to the new bar. Curious, we drew Bábá's attention to it.

“The old soap became so thin that I found it hard to hold,” He said. “So I stuck it on to the new one, and made it usable.”

That explanation taught us a lesson. Yet a question remained in our minds. Would it have done any harm to throw away that small piece of soap? It was not as if there was any lack of soap. One doesn't have to search far for the answer. What seemed trivial to us was important to Him because of His principle that one should not waste anything of this physical universe. We all have a personal responsibility to see that the limited resources of our planet remain available to all created beings.

In Bábá's case there was a second reason just as important. He owned nothing personally and all that He had was given to Him with love by His devotees and disciples. He would not throw anything away unless it was completely used up, and made certain that whatever was given to Him was carefully taken care of. He honoured all the loving offerings of His devotees.

The same thing occurred with the tubes of toothpaste or shaving cream. It was impossible for Him to part with them before they were finished. He discarded them only when He was sure the tubes were fully emptied.

In jail He suffered from colds time and again, so He kept a Vicks inhaler. One day we noticed that the inhaler had run out of medicine and had become useless. We managed to obtain a new inhaler from outside. But our efforts made little difference because He had meanwhile repaired the old one.

He had extracted the small inner tube from the inhaler, unrolled the cotton stuck to the upper end, pasted a small amount of Vicks “Vapo-rub” menthol ointment onto it and re-inserted the cotton into the tube and put it all back into the inhaler. The gadget got its life back and its efficacy was almost the same as that of a new one.

Bábá went on using the old inhaler without showing any interest in the new one. A month or so after this incident, we requested Bábá to discard the old inhaler and use the new one. We pointed out that however efficacious the old one might be, it could not be as good as the new one. Brushing our arguments aside, Bábá said, “Why, it's doing a fine job. I'll use the new one only when this one is completely exhausted.”

One day we were cleaning. We moved the table; the inhaler dropped to the floor and broke into pieces. Only then did Bábá begin to use the new inhaler. But He grumbled that He could have continued using the old one if it had not broken.

The inhaler was a small cheap thing, but its usability was by no means little to Him. The question of throwing the old inhaler away as long as it was usable did never arise. Bábá was so relentless about the full utilization of things that He thought it unpardonable to improperly use something or to allow it to be thrown away before had it lost all its value.

Another time the *cappals* (sandals) which Bábá used in the cell became drab and ugly from long and constant use. So when we proposed a new pair of sandals, Bábá demurred and said, “No, not now, I have no difficulty in using them. I shall replace them should the necessity arise.” Needless to say, He did not permit us to bring in a pair of new *cappals*.

One December the cold was bone-chilling. The woollen shirt Bábá usually wore was a few years old, and it no longer fit well. We managed to have a new woollen shirt brought and requested Bábá to use it instead of the old one. He refused, saying, “It doesn't matter if it doesn't fit all that well. Don't you see that I use it at night under my shawl?” So we failed to make Him part with that old shirt.

There was another occasion one July. After finishing evening *sádhaná*, He took His habitual walk in front of the cell. That was the only time He wore shoes. In the cell He wore *cappals*. At that time there were two pair of shoes—one old pair which had been with Him since He entered the jail and another new pair bought only a few days back. On rainy days when the path became muddy, He would put on the old pair.

He said, “My new ones will be ruined if I use them on a muddy street. On the other hand, it is a must to use the new shoes on a dry street because if the shoes are not broken in, one is likely to suffer from blisters.” The lesson being that one must use things in such a way that they are used to their fullest.

The food sent in for Bábá twice a day was always plenty. As Bábá ate little a large part was left over.

One day we were speaking with Bábá. Suddenly He asked, “Well, my boys, you don't let the food go to waste, do you?” We did not know what to reply, nor could we find words adequate to vent our emotion. We considered it a special blessing to be able to share Bábá's food.

We said simply, “No.”

Bábá said, “That's fine. No food should be wasted.” He then changed subjects.

Bábá could never come to terms with any kind of waste, let alone throwing away precious food. Although He knew that the food was never wasted, He asked the question to make us aware of His view. I remember that once before He had warned us not to waste food under any circumstances. Many times He told us to distribute any leftovers to the poor prisoners and scavengers who were ill-paid and ill-fed. He reminded us that it was a part of our service to humankind. We were confined and limited in our capacity to do much service, but He taught us that nothing should obstruct our service to humanity.

# CHAPTER 5

## ACTION MAKES ONE GREAT

Shankaracharya once said, *Mokśakárańa samagryáḿ bhaktireva gariiyasii* (“Of all the ways to attain salvation, *bhakti*, devotion, is the greatest”). Bábá underscored devotion as the main factor for progress on the spiritual path. Devotion is the essence of life. He said that life without devotion is a desert; life becomes tasteless, like food without salt.

Although Bábá attached primary importance to devotion, He did not lessen the importance of doing proper action (*karma*) in the world. He said, *Karma Brahmeti karma bahu kurviita* (“Work is *Brahma*; therefore work more and more”), and again, in a *vánii*, “It is action which makes one great. Be great by your *sádhaná*, your service and your sacrifice.”

### Incredible Dynamism

In the 1950's, Ananda Marga was in its beginning phase. At that time there were no wholetime workers, *ácáryas* or *avadhútas*,[^1] nor were there any books. It was through Bábá's untiring efforts that Ananda Marga's philosophy was published. He was a government employee then. Although the writing of the badly needed books was time-consuming, He did not allow the activities of His newly-created mission to interfere with His government job. For months on end He worked continuously on the books, and in this way many

were brought out within a few years.

That was in Jamalpur. He did His daily work and was not free until after evening *sádhaná*. Around eight or eight-thirty He went out for a stroll and stayed out about ten. In the quiet nights of Jamalpur village, He would walk with some devotees through the meadow to the tiger’s grave and talk about so many things. They would sit there and Bábá would speak on different subjects. Some devotees would write it down carefully. They brought with them electric torches to light up their notes in the dark surroundings. In this way *Ánanda Sútram* was written. He used to dictate after eating dinner, and would continue until two or three in the morning. During the lunch break at work, He would sit with some devotees and use the extra time to dictate and correct manuscripts. All the early books were written in this way.

Bábá never procrastinated. Quoting the saint Kabir, He would say, “Do today what you have determined to do tomorrow. Do now what you have decided to do today. At any time you may leave this body, and then, when will you do it?”

His speed was so fast-paced that it was difficult for us to keep up. This was felt especially towards the end of the 1980's when a phase of heavy construction began at Anandanagar. Bábá envisioned Anandanagar as developing into a spiritual hub of the world. He sketched out His vision and had us begin the foundation work in making Anandanagar into a huge complex of educational and research facilities in all fields of spirituality, agriculture, science, and culture.

He set everything into motion to bring this spectacular work to its proper completion. The educational institutions, ranging from kindergartens to colleges, came into existence one after the other at lightning speed. We built reservoirs, water drainage systems, afforested barren hills and created animal sanctuaries, to bring about a revolutionary change in the land and biosphere. We were participating in a dream come true to change the environs of Anandanagar into a future garden city. Bábá was determined to conquer time and complete the foundation plans which normally would take years. He allowed no excuses for delay, though we ran out of breath to keep pace with Him.

### Presence of Mind and Farsightedness

The growth of Ananda Marga has never been plain sailing. We have faced all kinds of opposition, hostility and brutal attack from those who wished to extinguish the light Ananda Marga shone on the world. Sometimes the situations we faced were so complex that we spent hours in vain trying to sort them out. When we were finally compelled to go to Bábá, He offered the perfect solutions to every situation. His insight was so clear that no problem was a problem to Him. He used to say that however huge problems appear to be, the mind possesses a greater capacity to solve them. So one should not shy from the apparent size or number of crises one faces.

In 1969, Bábá left India to visit the Philippines. The local devotees were delighted and did their best to make the *dharma mahácakra* a success. Unfortunately, back in India, Anandanagar was passing through an ordeal. Some politically-backed ruffians were threatening to attack the *jágrti*, terrorizing the Margis into fleeing. They were harassing the local devotees in public, and their threats could not be ignored. At a loss as to what to do, the workers sent an SOS to our Calcutta office. As there was no alternative, we called the Philippines and Bábá was informed of everything. Bábá

immediately replied, “Don’t worry. Keep in mind, ‘A dog’s bark is worse that its bite.’ Nothing will happen there. But that does not mean that they should not remain united and prepared.”

In the end, those hooligans did not do anything. Who can say that their plans were not thwarted by an invisible hand? Though far away from the scene, Bábá rightly read the minds of those ruffians and was able to guide us to safe action.

### Prioritizing Work

In Prabhát Saḿgiita, there is a *Jhumur*-style song that sings, “work sustains us”. Bábá drew sustenance from work; He did not let a moment go by without work. Clearly the missionary workers of Ananda Marga should always be engaged in working for society. With so much to do for humanity they should never waste their time. One’s work may be small or big, but the all-round success of it depends on a systematic approach. A job which is apparently minor may really be of great significance. If one neglects something because it seems unimportant, one may face difficult consequences as a result. So your work should be planned carefully.

Bábá usually plunged Himself into the ocean of work. He delighted in it. It is the nature of the mind that it does not like to remain vacant. Even when we sleep, our brains are busy. The thoughts that haunt our minds may be good or bad, creative or destructive. So a wise person will engage himself or herself in doing good works that will channel his or her thoughts and keep the mind exalted.

### *Sádhaná* and Duty Should not Be Jumbled Up

Bábá kept a fixed routine for His work and laid equal emphasis on a fixed routine for *sádhaná*. But He did not like one to do one’s duty at the cost of *sádhaná*, nor the other way around. He was dismayed if He saw someone doing *sádhaná* and neglecting his duty.

It was sometime in January or February 1966. Bábá was living at Anandanagar. We worked day in and day out constructing new buildings. We were working without respite. It was quite possible that some of us let our *sádhaná* slip on the excuse of a heavy workload. Bábá certainly knew of this, and perhaps that is why He ordered all workers to do collective *sádhaná* every morning and evening, ensuring our routine was kept. Then immediately after *sádhaná*, we would eat a little and go to work.

Bábá kept the schedule tight. At ten o’clock one morning, He caught sight of one worker doing *sádhaná* in the *jágrti* and flew into a rage. He scolded, “Why *sádhaná* now? It’s time for work. Tell him that the time for *sádhaná* and the time for work must not be jumbled up.” We hastened to arouse that *dádá* from *sádhaná*. He then joined in the activity.

“Humans have come to earth for a limited time only,” Bábá once said. “One won’t get time to do great deeds or achieve success in life unless one learns to make the most of each moment.” Bábá ensured that every moment was utilized completely. We had not to be reminded of schedules more than once.

### Jail Could not Stop the Work

Bábá’s love for work found its outlet even in jail. Con-

finement had little impact on Him. But what should we do with the vast stretch of time on hand? Daily, about a dozen newspapers and journals came in from outside, though He did not take very long to read them through. We were limited in every respect and failed in our various efforts to fill our enforced leisure. But Bábá came up with solutions. He filled the hours reciting poems, singing songs, and we acted out dramas invented by Him. We thoroughly enjoyed ourselves. At the same time, His endless knowledge and resourcefulness never ceased to amaze us. The days glided by.

And the nights? Bábá was occupied until nine o’clock with *sádhaná* and His evening routine. Often Bábá ate dinner at twelve or one o’clock, for His poor health decreased His appetite, and we would patiently wait until He would eat. Then He went to bed. But then He would ask us to sing devotional songs to pass the time every night. Due to our limited repertoire – none of us being very great singers – we would sing more or less the same songs every night. But Bábá enjoyed them thoroughly. He praised us, then said, “Now tell me some good news.”

He wanted to hear some news about the organization. We had to prepare for this every day, though we didn’t have much to say because we, too, were out of touch with the latest news. Naturally, we frequently faltered, but, far from discouraging us, He filled in the gaps Himself and enlightened us about different aspects of our reports. It was after this that He went to bed and so did we.

Our sleep was no more than a light trance. We stayed alert in case He awoke and needed us. We knew that He would not call us if He saw we were asleep, so we lay with our eyes closed and our ears open. Through the night we counted the hours, listening to the watch guard’s hourly gong. At 4:30 we got up. When I would look towards Bábá’s cot, I would see Him lying awake. He seemed to be relieved to see me get up.

Many mornings He said, “Well, I’ll tell you something that I have been thinking about for a while. Please take down the notes.”

Though Bábá was lying down, He never really slept. It was in the serenity of the night that He would draw up new plans and programmes for the organization and for the welfare of humanity. In the early hours, He would ask me whether a book which we had sent to the printers had been published yet. He would eagerly await news about the relief work of AMURT. He would be anxious about a junior worker who had been sent to a remote place. After making inquiries, He would dictate new programmes for the mission’s welfare activities. He was satisfied as I took down His instructions carefully. In this way we passed the nights and early mornings. Soon the red streaks of dawn light were in sight. The iron door of the cell was thrown open and we began a new day.

### *Samyak Karmánta*

Lord Buddha laid great emphasis on *Samyak Karmánta*. Bábá’s whole life was *Samyak Karmánta*, or Right Action. Bábá’s sense of responsibility, by nature, was remarkable. Be it his personal affairs, organizational matters or official position, He was extremely disciplined and methodical and would not tolerate any laxity. It was His habit to complete the first job on hand before taking up another.

Once late in the seventies, Bábá was talking to us. He was in good humour and was speaking about some events of His life when He still worked in Jamalpur in the railways office. As a part of His duties, He now and then had to inspect

different offices and stores of the departments to check on their work and accounts.

Bábá was ordered to go inspect a particular store of the railway department. He thought to give intimation of His visit to the stores officer. Accordingly, He phoned the railway store. A subordinate answered the call. Bábá wanted to know if that was the official speaking. Being told it was so, Bábá said, “This is Prabhat Ranjan Sarkar speaking from headquarters, I am coming tomorrow for an inspection of your stores. Please ask your officer-in-charge to come to the phone and talk to me about it.” Bábá’s words came like a thunderbolt over the telephone line.

Unable to control himself, he shouted through the receiver, “My God! Prabhat Ranjan Sarkar? Mr. Sarkar coming for inspection!” The man’s voice trembled. From His end of the phone, Bábá could detect the man’s fear. The man dropped the receiver and ran to his supervisor to give the message. The supervisor went to the phone and identified himself. Bábá was a bit annoyed.

He asked the officer, “Well, what is the matter? Your man seemed to be scared at the sound of my name – as if he had had a heart attack. Am I a tiger?”

The officer apologized for his subordinate’s behaviour. He requested Bábá to come as scheduled. The very idea that Shrii Sarkar was making an inspection was sufficient to make some people nervous.

Bábá told us, “As you all know, whenever I went for inspection the first thing I would do would be to ask for certain specific files and documents. That, however, would be fatal for them, for I would ask for the very files and documents which had not been maintained properly. There I would find all the serious discrepancies and irregularities. A lot of time and energy would have been unnecessarily wasted if I had had to search through so many papers and files in the whole office.

Their efforts to mislead me with false statements, duplicate documents, got-up cases, etc., would fail miserably. Moreover, I never accepted anything, neither kind or cash, from anyone, nor did I take any food offered from those under inspection. If I had, it would have obligated me to act in their interest, which I could never do.

“Now, people knew me very well, and when their scandalous acts of fraud were exposed by me, they would break down in despair and appeal for mercy: ‘Mr. Sarkar, I was forced by extreme poverty and pressure of circumstances to do it. Please do not fire me from my job! Please! I have a very big family to maintain. They will die of starvation, sir.’”

Bábá continued, “I would be in a dilemma. On the one hand, I could not go against my conscience favour to a guilty man. On the other hand, could I take steps that would throw the poor fellow out of employment and be the cause of making his family starve? Of course, cases of termination of service rarely occurred during my service life. Unless it was absolutely impossible to save the offender without going against my responsibilities, I never did anything that might throw a man out of a job. Really, I did feel for these poor fellows. So, in most cases, I would advise them what and how to save their faces and retain their jobs without, at the same time, any loss on the part of the department concerned.”

Bábá said in a saddened voice, “Again and again, I had to face similar, painful situations. To continue with official duties and to have pity and sympathy for the poor are not consistent with each other. So I decided to leave my government service in order to devote myself fully to the mission

work of organization in the greater interests of the people in general. So I resigned.”

### Work While Dying

I do not know how often Bábá repeated, “It is good to die working, of course.” Then He would add, “But better still to do work while dying.”

He also said that it was necessary to accomplish a task neatly. This is part of *Samyak Karmánta*. He was strict that one should be responsible in fulfilling one’s duties; He followed this principle in His own life.

In May 1970, in Muzaffarpur, Bihar, we held a *dharma mahácakra* (DMC).\* I knew Bábá was suffering from piles and was bleeding profusely, so He found it difficult to sit properly. He was the centre of attention and the DMC entirely depended on Him. Yet I saw that His discomfort did not affect His mood or stop Him from going through every part of the DMC.

The main attraction of a DMC is a discourse in which He gives His special blessing to the massive gathering of devotees. He took His seat on the dais, and, leaning against a pillow, delivered a long discourse. Only a few of us had any idea that Bábá was severely suffering. He endured His pain with a smile and did everything connected with the DMC. Even in the face of affliction, He would not deviate from His routine.

### Proper Finishing of Work

Not only was Bábá thorough in carrying out His duties, but He completed each task to perfection. He was extremely methodical, and whatever He did, He did with a definite purpose and reason behind it. There was always rational method in His thoughts and actions.

For instance, when assigning a worker to a place, one should take into consideration his or her qualifications, background and personality—his or her strong points as well as weak ones. He could properly evaluate every worker from so many points of view and then assign them appropriate duties because of His tremendous insight. He also involved and consulted the different supervisors, taking their suggestions or pointing out flaws in their arguments, before making a final decision. His logic was invariably irrefutable, and everyone was happy with the solutions offered. No one else could ever be as thorough to the extent that Bábá was. That is why Bábá personally supervised the assignment of workers up to His last day.

He would paint a graphic picture of the geography and environment. book. Moreover, Bábá would give some instructions as to how to work in a certain field. He often made a detailed programme, sending an experienced worker with the new one to the field, setting the date of departure and giving advice about the initial set-up. He would then ascertain whether we had a clear idea of our specific jobs and what we were expected to do.

"Yes, Bábá."

"Do you have any questions or doubts about any aspect?"

"No, Bábá."

"Can you repeat all that you've understood?"

In this way Bábá personally completed perfectly His task of training us. He worked in the spirit of *Samyak Karmánta* and expected the same from us. He often said, "I'm habituated to be always engrossed in work. I won't put up with negligence of duty or procrastination." He would recite the Sanskrit verse:

> *Nidrá tandrá bhayaḿ krodhaḥ álasyaḿ diirghasútratá,*
> *Hátavyáḥ ete śadadośáḥ bhútimicchatá*

He worked at lightning speed, yet He worked perfectly. We found it hard to keep pace with Him and sometimes we floundered. He loved us dearly, yet He was not one to ignore mistakes made out of carelessness. He scolded us and loved us so that we might move quickly towards perfection.

CHAPTER 6

# CAREFUL PLANNING AND SPEEDY EXECUTION

One night some time in 1969 Bábá and I were walking in the garden of His house. He took a notebook from His pocket, skipped over a few pages, and then handed it to me. Each page was filled with words and phrases. I stared at it, but barring a few words, I could not make head nor tail of those jottings. I could see it was written in some kind of code. Bábá asked if I understood what I saw, but I looked blank. Then Bábá explained, "I write down in shorthand my future plans and programmes, what and when I will do them, and when I will introduce them into the organization. I give shape to my ideas at the right time."

Bábá never acted impulsively. All the work was thought out thoroughly. A brief glance at Ananda Marga's development from the start makes it clear that all the activities were closely and strongly connected with one another. It's as though the different phases of Ananda Marga's growth, the new philosophical theories and other programmes, were waiting in the wings of the theatre to appear on-stage at the appropriate time.

### Prabhát Saḿgiita

Bábá was fond of music, especially devotional songs. Before Prabhát Saḿgiita was composed, Bábá enjoyed listening to the songs of the devotees, whether they were in literary language or not. Even songs that were monotonous or lacked poetic rhythm could send Him into a reverie. In the early days some of the songs sung by the devotees in Bihar were, to my ears at least, only mixture of deafening drums, noisy clapping and ear-splitting tuneless singing. Nevertheless Bábá would be lost in a trance of pleasure. He used to say that though the songs bordered upon cacophony there was a spontaneous flow of devotion. It was the devotional feeling that made Bábá happy and applaud the singers.

On 14 September 1982 Bábá composed the first song of Prabhát Saḿgiita in the pleasant natural environment of Deoghar. Within a few years He composed a stunning number of songs – over five thousand. It was mind-boggling to witness Him create song after song. He created a new genre of songs throbbing with emotion and rhythm; they were a synthesis of oriental and occidental themes and a brilliant exposition of *rágas* and *ráginiis*. Bábá created a new realm of music.

Actually, I know nothing about music, let alone have any ability to sing. But surprisingly, Bábá included me in the team of note-takers learning Prabhát Saḿgiita from Him. It cannot be said that any other members of the team had much knowledge either, but the absence of a trained singer in the group did not stop Him from getting the songs down right. It reminds me of the saying that God can make the dumb speak and the lame walk. Bábá bestowed His grace on us and made the impossible possible.

Once Bábá came to Anandanagar for DMC. It was a beautiful morning. After having made a song to a very sublime *rága*, He was eating breakfast. Suddenly He said to me. "The tune I used today I once used in an Urdu song that I composed in my school days. Would you like to hear it?"

"Oh yes, please," I said. Bábá sang that Urdu song. I was spellbound by the lyrics and the melody. Though the theme of the song was social and lacked spiritual content, I liked

it very much.

Bábá added, “I composed many poems in those days.”

I said, “You composed such marvelous songs when you were a student, but it seems you have waited a long time before beginning Prabhát Saḿgiita. Why is that?”

Bábá explained smilingly, “I have told you that I always act at the proper time in accordance with the plans I’ve made. Let us say that I have a cow that gives an immense amount of milk. Should I start milking it right away, or should I first see to it that I’ve a pot to put the milk in? If I start milking without a pot, all of it will spill to the ground and be lost. So, I had a plan to create something special in the world of music that would be a unique fusion of devotion and exuberance to bring about a great spiritual vibration all over the world. But until now there was no proper medium to contain that vibration. It was only when I was convinced that the right people were there and the infrastructure existed to sustain and utilize those spiritual vibrations that I was able to start Prabhát Saḿgiita with the first song, *Bandhu He Niye Calo* (‘O Friend, Guide Me’).”

Bábá was able to do much because He followed a strict routine – every moment filled with useful work. He was punctual, He ordered His time carefully and He expected us to keep pace with Him. If there was any slackness in our speed He would be very dissatisfied and take us to task. He did not accept any excuses for delays. When I think about it now, I cannot imagine how we accomplished so much in a few short years. Now I believe it was because Bábá knew His physical presence among us soon would come to a close and there was so much yet to be done.

Seeing Bábá compose day in and day out those 5018 songs, on top of all the other work He did, awed me. I was amazed at how He could work non-stop through His meal-times, bath times, on field walks, or lying down. At any moment He would call us in to take down some dictation of a book or a newly-composed song or an organizational directive.

In jail Bábá once said with anguish, “Imprisonment doesn’t affect me. In fact, I find little difference between confinement and liberty. The uneasiness which torments me is because I don’t find scope here to immerse myself in the ocean of activity. I have endless things to do, for our organization has spread all over the world and every moment is valuable. But as I have no chance to make the most of every minute, I am languishing here.”

### The Efficient Housewife

He once explained in an elegant illustration that it is an art to do everything neatly. The more carefully one’s work is planned, the more successful the outcome.

He said, “Suppose a housewife is to help her husband by preparing his lunchbox before he leaves for work in the morning. With this end in view, the first thing she is required to do after rising is kindle the coal stove. By the time the fire is ready, she has completed straightening up the rooms and washing the cooking utensils. She puts a pot of water on and she knows when it will boil so that she can add the rice. By the time the rice has boiled she has finished her bath and replaced the pot of rice with another pot for the pulse. While the pulse is cooking, she cuts the vegetables so that she can fry them after the pulse is ready. Thus she completes her cooking in good time and hands her husband his lunchbox.”

This is how one should plan one’s work, otherwise everything will become confused and the result will be chaos.

Then all one’s hard effort come to nothing, and important work is left half-done. If one wants to be efficient, one must plan for it and stay determined not to allow those plans to fall apart. To be successful one must keep an eye on priorities and plan well.

Bábá would often remind us that life is short. We should make the most of the short span allotted to us and not remain idle. Work unceasingly and with sincerity, and your life will be worth living. If any of us were inactive, Bábá would be highly displeased with us. He often said in more or less the same words, “When after innumerable births in the inexorable cycle of creation, one gets a human body, one should leave behind a footprint which will be as lasting as a line scored in rock. If since our birth we attend to our physical body for the sake of mere survival and if we drag our existence through some feelings of weal and woe until we die, we shall only leave behind a line drawn on water – or sand at best. Those who die such a death are remembered by their kith and kin for a brief period, then they are lost in oblivion forever. This is what happens in most cases.”

We ought to take as example those who have dedicated their lives to the service of humanity, utilized each and every moment of their lives, tried to do in ten minutes what is usually done in ten days, and thus got their names written in golden letters in the pages of history.

### Irresistible Working Speed

In 1982 our Central Office was going to be built in the suburb of Tiljala in Calcutta. We conceived of several multi-storied buildings: one for Central Office, a three-storied building for Bábá’s residence, and the Central Office for WWD at a little distance away. The construction of all three buildings was to go on at the same time. The entire programme would require over Rs. 10,000,000, though we had only a few thousand rupees at our disposal.

Bábá summoned me, the office secretary, Ác. Prańavánanda Avt., and the construction secretary, the late Ác. Girijánanda Avt., and said, “How long will you take to complete all the tasks?”

We knew Bábá’s irresistible speed and we knew that He would not listen to any excuses that would delay construction. We vested our hopes for a feasible deadline in a roundabout statement.

I said, “Bábá, it is a very big project; they are big buildings. Had it been a government project it would take over six years to complete. The engineers we consulted think that construction can be completed in three years provided we work at great speed. Our own Margi engineers, however, knew that you would not consider such a long target date and we think that if we put in hard work round the clock we can complete the project within a year and a half.”

Our hearts were already beating fast after I had made this statement about the one-and-a-half-year deadline. On everyone’s mind was that the time frame so bravely mentioned was likely to elude us on account of a paucity of funds. We would be pushed to the limits to keep our word. But any worries in this vein did not have a chance to proceed further as Bábá started thundering.

He looked at the others who were there and said scathingly of us, “You see, these fellows are completely devoid of wisdom. They have all tightened their belts only to exasperate me. It is a small project – only a few buildings. Why should it entail a huge expenditure? Just see, they are telling me that the project will take a year and a half. What a joke! Are they without common sense?”

Turning to us He said, “Get this straight: you must complete it within six months, and not six years, three years or even a year and a half.”

We were dumbfounded. We had neither the courage nor the foolishness to say that it was impossible. An ordinary man would have had a heart attack from the shock. But we were fairly acquainted with Bábá’s method of work. He got impossible tasks done by exerting unusual pressure. So without the slightest murmur of protest, we acquiesced and swung into action. We geared up the speed of our activities so that we might not have to face Bábá. In spite of our non-stop hard work, Bábá took us to task during our reporting sessions with Him, which we had three times daily.

Sometimes, especially after a rough reporting session, we thought that enough was enough. On the one hand we were hamstrung by the lack of funds, and on the other, we were being badgered by the suppliers. We were working day and night with little rest, sleep or food. We were over our ears in bills, yet we had to report our progress to Bábá thrice daily with no ray of hope of getting money from any corner.

This crisis reached a peak of intolerable worry for me. I went to my office and locked the door. I was sitting there staring at the wall, dismal, with a long face. “What shall I do, what shall I do?” I wondered over and over again. I could not think of any way out of our financial bind.

Someone had come and was repeatedly knocking on the door. I composed my face in haste and opened the door. A Margi was standing at the threshold.

He said, “I have come to you, Dádájii.” Seeing my sad face he added, “Are you indisposed?”

“Not exactly. What can I do for you?”

“I have come as a messenger of Shrii K—— to hand this money over to you for Bábá’s project.” Then he happily handed me a bundle of notes. The money was so unexpected that I was completely overwhelmed. I had no idea how much he was giving — but whatever, it would undoubtedly help tide us over in the prevailing crisis. Immediately I remembered what Bábá had once told us, “The pressure of work will go on increasing as the organization expands. Your need for funds will also be aggravated. That will trigger a lot of agony and anxiety. Yet if there is sincerity plus dynamism, money will never pose a problem. The want of money will always shadow you, but it will never stand in the way of a project you have undertaken.” And in fact the buildings were habitable, even if they lacked a few finishing touches, within six months.

Tears welled up in my eyes as I realized that His eyes were always on me even when I was thinking depressing thoughts holed up in my room. Once again it became clear that He would do His work in the face of opposition; we have only to depend on Him faithfully, and go on with our duties with iron determination. Success will surely come, but we must jettison something in exchange. We must throw away our egoism. I felt that it was only complete surrender to Him that could bring us to the completion of our mission.

There is a song I like in Prabhát Saḿgiita: “Let me weep for You, if You derive joy by making me weep.” He puts us into trouble and takes us out also; all to learn surrender of the ego. In this case, the tension and anxiety which we had been thrown into defies description, but the sudden happy rescue made me realize once again that it was His grace that always helps us through all ordeals.

This tremendous speed characterized all of Bábá’s work, particularly in the late 1980’s as He churned out one programme after another in order to lay the foundation plans of the dream city of Anandanagar. Without rest or respite, He

continued giving hundreds of projects, lending no ear to excuses or delays, whether or not we could accomplish the projects, whether or not they were feasible. Lying awake until two or three in the morning, He planned new projects, then would summon me and other workers before daybreak. We noted down His instructions about how Anandanagar was to be built as an ideal master unit and immediately set to work on them, but we were always baffled by the constant fund crunch.

At the time none of us could understand why Bábá insisted on doing everything at supersonic speed, nor had we wisdom to foresee that a countdown had already begun for a rocket to carry the Unknown Traveller to a distant unfamiliar place. Everything was a hurried preparation for His flight into infinity. We could hardly be expected to know of those plans. We did not have the insight to read the signs that He was getting ready to conclude His earthly sojourn.

# NEITHER BORROWER NOR LENDER BE

There was one time when Bábá scolded me sufficiently that I learned a lesson to last me for life.

In the *Mahábhárata*, Yudhisthira had an encounter with Dharma disguised as a *yakśa* (spirit). Testing him the *yakśa* asked, “Who is the happiest person on earth?”

Yudhisthira answered, “The happiest person is one who eats a simple diet at the end of a day’s toil, who is not over his or her head in debt, and who need not migrate abroad to earn a living.”

Bábá certainly agreed with Yudhistira’s second point. He was against people borrowing or lending money. One day he said, “You must know that lending as well as borrowing is undesirable and detestable.”

I had known Bábá’s attitude and so had always tried not to borrow money. But I still had a question, “Was it so bad to lend money to someone in distress?”

Bábá answered with an illustration. “Do you know why it isn’t desirable to lend out money? Suppose someone has come to you to borrow some money. Maybe his need is genuine and his appeal is justified. As you listen to his story of distress, you are likely to want to be kind to him. Even if it is beyond your own means to lend a big amount, you become so upset thinking of his dire need that you take pity on him. You begin to think of how you will manage to get such a large sum for him. But, you cannot drive anxiety out of your mind as to whether you will get your money back quickly. Your thoughts get interrupted as the gentleman says, ‘You need not worry, dear brother. Please help me

wriggle out of my crisis. If your generosity can take me out of the present crisis, I'll repay the loan within a month. Meanwhile I'm likely to get some money soon, so you may rest assured that you'll get back your money on time.’ ”

Bábá continued, “There may be no reason for you to disbelieve him. Suppose you are soft-hearted, so you collect the money somehow and hand it over to him. And he, too, feels glad and goes away.

“That man may not have any intention of taking you for a ride, but it may turn out that he can't get hold of the money as he thought. It is not so unusual. But you go on nursing the hope that he will repay you at the end of the month.

“A month goes by. He hesitates to see you out of his shame because he has no money to repay to you. Suppose you go to him out of need for the money. In all likelihood he will be ill at ease and may take offence at your uncalled-for presence. He will think, ‘How disgusting! He has come to me straightway.’ Understandably, he will tell you a long excuse about why he has failed to repay you. You will give a patient hearing to him and coming back home begin to wait for the deadline as promised. There will be times when you come across him on the street. Immediately he will think, ‘O my God! Here he is again, he will certainly remind me of my debt!’

“Of course it's only natural such thoughts come to mind, interspersed with some inaudible abusive remarks. Maybe you didn't go to him for money, but what is that to him? Your presence will be unpleasant enough.

“So whenever you meet him, he will think, ‘I am lost! He has come again to pressure me for that money.’ At the same time he will hurl some unsavoury remarks at you, albeit silently. Contrary to what is in his mind, he will say, ‘Welcome, my dear brother. I am glad to meet you. I am so grateful to you,’ and so on and so forth.

“Undoubtedly the moment he sees you, he will heap uncharitable remarks on you, but when you will come within earshot, he will be all praises and parade his respect for you. It goes without saying that you will be put into a dilemma from not getting your money back even at the time of your own dire need. It may happen that your need will drive you to his house and shout from outside, ‘Hello Mr. —, are you there?’

“He will think, ‘Oh! he has come again for the money. What will I say to him?’

“Thinking this, he will not only silently curse you, but also try his best to avoid an encounter with you. Instead of coming outside, he will tell his little son to tell you that his father is out. Accordingly, the boy will run to you to inform you that his father is not in.

“Looking askance at him, you will say, ‘Go inside and find out for certain whether or not your father is there.’ The simple-minded child will reply, ‘Why, I don't need to check because Father has himself said that he is out.’ In this way your relationship with that friend will sour and you don't know where it will end.

“Well, he certainly would have been offended that first day if you had not lent him money. Not only that, he would possibly have been shocked and have silently heaped some abusive remarks. Nevertheless, the unpleasant situation would not have spilt over onto the rest of your life. If you lend money, you will inevitably invite filthy remarks galore. That is why a person should think many times before lending to others.”

I asked, “Sometimes a really good man may fall into trouble and sincerely need help. In that case, what is to be done?”

Bábá said, “Instead of lending him money, you should help him according to your capacity. Suppose he is asking for five thousand rupees. Tell him your attitude about lending money and let him know your financial condition. Then help him with an amount that’s within your means. It may not be as much as he demands, but he will get consolation that you tried to share his difficulties.”

And so I received a lesson I will never forget. We were in Ranchi in 1969. It was then our camp headquarters. I was general secretary and attending a never-ending sessions trial in Midnapur, West Bengal, on the horrific murder of five of our monks (at Anandanagar, 5 March 1967) and a huge amount of money was being drained out of our treasury to pay the ongoing legal costs. Much would depend on the verdict of the court; we were fighting the case heart and soul. Our funds were almost exhausted, but we were only halfway through the case. I was at my wit’s end when an idea occurred to me. One of the mission’s departments had deposited about ten thousand rupees in the central treasury. They had planned to buy a car to use for social service. Left with no other way out, I sent a special messenger to Ranchi and fetched that money. We heaved a sigh of relief as we escaped the crisis for the time being.

The case came to a finish at last. The court sentenced eight of the culprits to life imprisonment and others to rigorous imprisonment for different terms. There was no denying it: the verdict vindicated dharma and demoralized those who had masterminded the heart-rending massacre at Anandanagar.

A few days later the workers from that department began to urge me to repay their money. I was on the lookout for resources, but my efforts were taking a long time. Eventually Bábá heard of the entire affair.

When He came to know of it, He took exception to it. He came to the office that day and called all the central workers of the different departments. He demanded an explanation from me.

Bábá asked, “Don’t you know the policy of our mission about lending and borrowing?”

“Yes, Baba,” I said.

“If that is so,” Bábá continued, “why didn’t you get permission from the president (that is, Bábá Himself) before siphoning the money from one department to another?”

“We were in extreme need at the time. I felt we had no other option than taking this loan. It was an emergency.”

“You ought to have informed the president of that emergency. Moreover, you could have taken the permission of the departmental head before spending the money.”

Bábá’s displeasure was extreme, and He scolded me severely for breaking the rules of financial propriety of the organization. My discomfiture was writ large on my face. I admitted my guilt and promised not to do it again in the future.

Decades have passed and much water has passed under the bridge since then, yet even now I can visualize the unrelenting image of that strict administrator – our loving Bábá. That scolding is burnt into my memory. That lesson has made me stand firmly on my feet and I have never thought again of borrowing money though the mission has scraped through countless difficulties over the years. Want of money was – and is, and always will be – there, but the virtual ban on borrowing has never stalled my work. For that matter I have never had to compromise on principles. His grace has helped me through every time.

Bábá told me a story of His childhood. When He was a

young student, He was friends with a boy named Apu. Apu’s father was a strict disciplinarian. He had framed a daily routine for his son. The boy dared not break it. In case he departed from it slightly, his father came down heavily on him.

Once Apu came home late and his father scolded him severely and punished him. The boy wept from his father’s cruelty. He could not understand why, unlike the fathers of his friends, his father was so ruthless.

One day he laid his heart bare to his friend, Prabhat-dá (Bábá). Bábá said to him, “This strict discipline will prove a blessing in your life. You may rest assured that you will shine in life.”

The consolation he got was enough for him to return home. In fact, Baba’s prediction came true. When he grew up, he excelled his classmates in every respect.

Later on Bábá met him and said, “Hello Apu, do you feel any difference between your reaction to your father in your boyhood and the attitude you have developed now?”

He said emotionally, “Oh yes, Prabhat-dá. At that time I thought my father was heartless. What I feel now is that had there been no strict discipline in that phase of my life, if my father had not been relentless about my daily routine, I would have lost myself in the wilderness. Whatever I have achieved is all due to my father.”

This story holds meaning for me. As I remember many moments of my life with Bábá, intense feelings flash across my mind. I feel that if Bábá had not played a strict role, we too, might have degenerated. If I now have any efficiency, vivacity, steadfastness or dynamism, it is due to only one cause, my beloved Bábá.

## LOVE FOR PLANTS AND ANIMALS

*Parama Puruśa* or Cosmic Consciousness is boundless, omnipresent and omniscient. It is He who sustains and governs the universe. The psycho-spiritual powers attained by *sádhakas* are all in His hand. Nothing moves, not even a blade of grass sways, against His will. He can do whatever He likes.

Devotees, however, do not accept this dry philosophical attitude. They feel the infinite *Parama Puruśa* is the nearest entity to their innermost being. So they figure that He is unable to do two things. First, He cannot create another like Himself, as the infinite cannot be divided into two. Secondly, He cannot hate any of His creation. A sinner may escape His wrath because *Parama Puruśa* always looks upon all of us as His very own. He takes the sinner close in His arms, and only admonishes him to stop his evil deeds. Just as you pick up a diamond from the mud and wash it clean, *Parama Puruśa* embraces the sinner because He cannot discriminate between His children.

Neglecting no one, He showers His endless love on all, animate and inanimate beings alike. Human beings are especially blessed with rationality and intuition. It is an eloquent testimony to His love for us.

### Ecological Balance

*Parama Puruśa* has given us a treasure house of infinite knowledge, but we often fail to make the best use of this wealth. Our selfishness and greed abuse the generosity of

*Parama Puruśa* and will inevitably bring nature’s retribution upon us. Moreover as the poet Rabindranath Tagore said, “Let Your wrath burn those who do wrong and those who tolerate wrong.” Those who commit wrong are sure to fall, but those who support them in their transgressions become partners in sin, and their fall, too, is certain.

One day Bábá said to me, “The subjects pay the penalty for the king’s offence.”

I asked, “Why should the innocent subjects bear the brunt of a king’s wrongdoing?”

Bábá said, “Yes, this is justice. A king who has done wrong must face trial and won’t be able to escape punishment. But the subjects cannot be absolved of responsibility, either, because they gave the king a free hand to do wrong.”

And who is not aware that such kings are the rulers of our present-day world? Our whole planet and its inhabitants must suffer from their greed. I do not know when this boiling cauldron of selfishness will be full to the brim, or when a new human society will emerge from its churning vortex.

Though human beings command a dominant position in this universe, *Parama Puruśa* does not favour humans at the cost of others. As everything – from ant to elephant, from an undeveloped amoeba to the mighty whale – is made by *Parama Puruśa*, He cannot love one less than another. Just as a mother has equal affection for all her children – even if one is physically handicapped or mentally retarded – so *Parama Puruśa* has love for all irrespective of caste, creed or colour – what to speak of species! There is no one who can say that he or she has been deprived of the boundless love of *Parama Puruśa*.

So, too, the birds and animals, plants and trees. Before their birth He provides them a favourable environment and a ready stock of food and water. For instance, in the early ages of our planet, in the Tertiary Age, about five to six million years ago there came into being some gigantic trees. Today’s spinach would have been as tall as a palm tree then. About a million years later, giant graminivorous animals such as the mammoth were able to feed on the descendants of those big plants. *Parama Puruśa* always follows a wonderfully impeccable system: creating the food and environment first before the creatures come into being.

Every living being has what it needs for its existence. *Parama Puruśa* has provided for it. Wherever we look we find tremendous variety and ingenuity in nature. Some animals have claws to tear meat while others have flat teeth for chewing grain. Roses have thorns for protection, and the deer speed. Some fly, others swim. Everything has been supplied by *Parama Puruśa*.

Each thing has its worth in this universe simply because *Parama Puruśa* created it; it has existential value. As Bábá once said, “Just as my life is dear to me, similarly the life of another creature is dear to it. Every entity loves its own existence. In the light of this truth, evolved human beings feel mercy for each and every object of this creation.” And every living thing also has some value because it plays some role in the intricate tapestry of the interdependence of life; it has utility value. Every being has its innate right to life; it has a right to enjoy water, air and the gifts that nature has provided for it.

Unfortunately, selfish people want to grab all they can at the expense of innocent creatures and plants. For instance, they butcher animals for their table. They are so self-centred that they feel no qualms of conscience in jeopardizing the rhythmic cycle of life. They do not care if birds and animals cease to exist and the ecological balance is destroyed.

*Parama Puruśa* created plants and animals to live in

harmony with human beings. We can ill afford to ignore nature’s inscrutable equilibrium, for we will have to pay for so doing in the end. Yet selfishness, avarice and cruelty has not spared the plants and trees, either. For instance trees play an important role in attracting rain. There are some trees whose root system holds and stores water. During dry seasons they release their water. Those riverside trees keep the rivers flowing all year round and the water table high. They keep the earth green, free from drought. The indiscriminate felling of trees and gradual disappearance of forest cover are posing a threat to human existence. Every tree negligently felled is but adding a plank to our own coffin.

### The Dancing Tree

It was a sunny, hot noontime in Manila, Philippines. Bábá had just finished a brief field walk and was sitting under a spreading tree. Some Margis were with Him. There was no breeze at all and the leaves were drooping and still. The devotees started singing *kiirtan* and Bábá was listening with joy. Suddenly some of the Margis noticed the leaves of the tree were quivering rhythmically.

Bábá smiled. “The leaves are happily dancing to the tune of the *kiirtan*,” He said. “They are as sensitive as people to joy and sorrow.”

That plants and trees feel pleasure and pain is a scientific fact. They yearn for a congenial environment and love. Placed in a positive environment, they grow quickly and healthily. If, on the contrary, they are trapped in a negative environment, their growth is stunted, the fruits are small and few. A tradition in some villages is when the people plant a tree they have a person known to be righteous come and plant it for them. They feel that his or her goodwill for the plants will aid its growth and the fruits will be plentiful. The fact is that the person’s loving mind has a subtle effect which helps a long way for a tree to grow well.

We have a few birch trees at Anandanagar. They were brought from the cold regions of the Himalayas. Instead of keeping them in the greenhouse, Bábá instructed that we put them in the nursery to be in the company of plants. They have taken root and seem to be in high spirits. In hot, dry Anandanagar, they are not getting what they would have got in the Himalayas, but they are getting the fine surroundings of a pleasant grove and the warm, caring touch of the gardeners. The natural camaraderie among the people, the devotional songs ringing through the day and the spiritual vibration generated by the *sádhaná* done there creates an atmosphere that helps the plants and trees to grow.

### Why Did That Leaf Fall?

Plants and trees feel happiness, dejection and sorrow, only they cannot express it in ways that people can understand. At Lake Gardens, Bábá frequently demonstrated that He knew the language of plants, and would be furious when they were inadvertently neglected by the gardeners.

Bábá used to walk around the garden every day after His field walk. There were thousands of plants and trees growing in that tiny space surrounding the house. Many were rare species brought from different countries. He would look at all the plants carefully and watch over their development. One day He saw that a leaf of a plant had fallen off. He flew into a rage.

“Who is responsible for watering the plants?” He thundered. “Whose job is it to supervise the gardening? Send for them immediately!”

Everyone dashed to the spot. Bábá was in a fury. He scolded, “This plant must not have been watered or nurtured properly. Otherwise why has it shed a leaf?” He charged them with irresponsibility and ineptitude. It was as though somebody had been murdered. Everyone admitted his fault and promised never to let it happen again.

In His room, Bábá let His temper cool.

He said to me, “Look, do you want to know why My heart bleeds at such a small thing? When a man is thirsty, he wants water and when hungry, he wants food. In the same way, a plant suffers from thirst if it doesn’t get water and reels in starvation if it doesn’t get the food it needs. People can express their feelings, but plants cannot. Plants have their own agonies which are invisible to us.”

“I understand their language,” He added, “and so get exasperated. Therefore I take all of you to task.”

### Peter, the Loving Dog

*Parama Puruśa*, the embodiment of love, inspires all created beings to love one another. Animals and plants have nothing but love in their hearts, but humans, with their developed egos, can hate as well as love.

I want to tell you about my dog Peter, and how affected he was by our love and actions. It was in 1984 that he lived with us. At that time I was the rector of Ánanda Shiila Master Unit (MU), in the district of Ranchi. The site was marvelous. The MU is enclosed in a triangle of streams, nestled among an expanse of greenery and far from noisy crowds. Being an isolated place, Mr. L.P. Agarwal of Ranchi made a gift of his German shepherd, Peter, to us to act as a watchdog. Big, black Peter looked as ferocious as a tiger, and his bark was as terrifying as a lion’s roar. Some zoologists say that a dog is a hybrid descended from a wolf and a fox. Our Peter had perhaps more wolf than fox in his blood. No one dared to come too close to him. Peter was also brave. One evening a calf from our dairy went towards the nearby jungle. A hyena attacked it and wounded its head. Peter rushed towards the spot and drove the hyena away and saved the calf.

However fearsome Peter might be to his enemies, he was very dear to us. He too loved us dearly. Whenever I put on my complete uniform, he understood that I was going away. He ran to the door, blocking my exit as if he did not want me to go. If I caressed him fondly and convinced him with a pretext saying, “If you let me go, I shall come back quickly with bread and biscuits for you,” he moved aside.

When I would come back after a few days, he ran to the gate to welcome me. He would jump up putting his paws on my shoulders. Anyone else would quake with fear to get such a greeting. No sooner did I pat him on the back than he downed his legs and led me, wagging his tail all the while. So sweet was his temperament that we all developed an intimate love for him.

Peter was particular about his diet. He had no taste for food other than milk and rice, and had a definite time to take his lunch and dinner. If his lunch was not served by 12:30 or 1:00, he would come gently from behind and put one of his front paws on my lap. By making some rumbling growls, he complained of his hunger. His reproach went straight to my heart. I hastened to the kitchen and reprimanded the staff for having forgotten to give Peter food. Peter, who followed me, joined in by growling. I could never imagine that kind of sensitivity in an animal before meeting Peter.

What was really surprising was his refusal to eat food on *ekádashiis*. Even if we brought a dish of food close to his mouth and tried to coerce him, he was visibly annoyed and

turned his muzzle away from the dish.

"Why will I take food?" Peter seemed to say. "It's fasting day. Perhaps I'm a dog, but humans can do it, why not me!"

There was another characteristic which endeared Peter to us. Whenever we performed *akhańda kiirtan* (continuous *kiirtan*) and sang our way around the central altar, Peter used to invariably hover around us. He could not join us in the *kiirtan* as God had not endowed him with the ability, yet he enjoyed the vibration emanating from the *kiirtan*. Thus, though he was a dog in appearance, he was part and parcel of the devotional life at our master unit.

Peter never gave me any chance to be peeved with him except once. One day I got really angry at him. It was the night of the new moon. At midnight I got ready to go out to perform my night *sádhaná*. As on previous occasions Peter accompanied me. I had no reason to object as he either sat at a distance from me or started for the *jágrti* as soon as I started preparations for my *sádhaná*. He had never interfered in my *sádhaná*. That night was different. I saw that he was still waiting there with no intention to leave. I tried my best to coax him away and leave me alone, but he would not go. Thinking that he would go away after a while, I sat down to perform my *sádhaná*.

The night was tranquil and quite dark. Dense jungles were all around, a thick pall lay over the entire area. A calm, desolate place is always helpful for a spiritual aspirant in withdrawing his or her mind from the external world and concentrating it in *sádhaná*.

This night I was completely absorbed, steeped in ecstasy. Suddenly I was rudely awakened – only to see that huge Peter had squirmed his way onto my lap. I was furious at him for disturbing my *sádhaná*. I said angrily, "Peter, I am doing my *sádhaná*. Leave me alone, won't you? Otherwise I'll drive you away with a severe thrashing."

But no, he would not leave me. As I was scolding him he was trying to say something through his whimpering growls. What was he trying to tell me? Why did he insist on staying on my lap? It came to me that perhaps he was tormented from within by an intense desire for *sádhaná*. But how could he possibly manage it in an animal body? As the time for my *sádhaná* was passing quickly, I could not wait any longer. I forcibly brushed him aside. It was only then that he started his trudge back to *jágrti*.

I concentrated on my *sádhaná* anew and came back to the *jágrti* very late. I had to rise at dawn as I would leave on tour to Varanasi and other places. When morning came and I was getting ready to leave, Peter rushed into the room, and before I could stop him, scooted under my cot. I tried to get him out, but it was in vain. The more I shouted at him, the more his mute protest. It was clear that he did not want me to go without him. Very peculiar, indeed.

I would miss the bus if I delayed any further. Left with no other option, some of the others forced him outside the room. His appeal touched me and it was with a heavy heart that I had to leave in that way. I was in two minds whether to part from him, but duty called. I left the *jágrti*.

I returned after about ten days. The others told me that Peter had been starving himself since I left. He had stopped barking and moving about, and had been lying all the while with tears twinkling down his eyes. I hastened to him. Patting him on the head, I said, "How is it Peter, that you have given up eating food?" He not only kept quiet, but turned his head away from me. It seemed that he certainly was offended at me. I was moved to see that he was still shedding tears.

I thought that maybe he had picked up an ailment. I hastily

ate some food and, as late as it was, took Peter in a rickshaw to the Khelari Veterinary Hospital accompanied by brother Arunodaya. Darkness was hovering all around. The doctor was not available just then. It was not until nine o'clock at night that he returned. He kindly took the time to conduct tests on Peter, but could not understand what he was suffering from. Nevertheless, he gave Peter some medicines and we felt a bit relieved. He did not charge us for the treatment.

When we were ready to leave the kind-hearted doctor insisted on taking us to his home, saying that it was unsafe to go through the jungle in the dead of night. He gave some milk to Peter, but all our efforts to make him drink proved futile. At last we offered our sincere gratitude to the doctor and lay down on our beds.

I did not sleep well that night. Scarcely did the light of the new day break when I rushed to see Peter. I stood aghast at the sight of Peter lying still. He was no more. Until now it was I who had traveled far and near, leaving him with cajoling pretexts. This time he had gone. Hurting my heart, he had left me forever. My eyes filled with tears and I regretted that once I had threatened to drive him away. "Strange are the ways of life," I thought. I consoled myself with the thought that every entity, be it a human being or a dog, finally tears the ties of love and affection and starts for an unknown world.

According to spiritual philosophy, an animal that comes in close contact with the higher mind of humans benefits by getting its own mind expanded and may leap forward to attain a human body in its next life. However, whether or not fortune would smile on Peter was to be decided by the Lord. Peter, like all animals and plants, what to speak of humans, yearned for sincere love and affection.

When I was a student there was a wry pun popular among us: *Duniyátá kár vash? Duniyá tákár vash* ("Who controls the world? Money controls the world"). There was a time I seriously thought it was true, but now I feel that nothing could be more hollow and less true. A poet once said, "It's only the brain, not the heart, that can be bought with money."

I now believe that to conquer a heart, one needs the kind of love that flows over everybody and onto everything. Only the one who pours out his or her affection on both the animate and inanimate creation can stand on the threshold of universal brotherhood. It is a prerequisite for *sádhaná*. Gurudeva once wrote: "A country can be conquered by force of arms, but the mind cannot. Those who have taken to *sádhaná* to conquer the mind are the true soldiers."

CHAPTER 9

# UNDAUNTED SPIRITEDNESS

Sublime ideals are the vehicle of greatness in life. Your success is assured when you identify with your ideals and act with sincere determination to reach the ultimate goal. The resulting moral courage is so great that no external force will be able to stop you. You will be able to move along your course of action, undeterred by the obstacles placed before you by your opposition.

Whenever anyone tries to establish a righteous cause, opposing forces are sure to attempt to stop them. This is certainly true in the day to day life of everyone, and it is especially true in the great movements of history. A look into the pages of history shows us that all great men and women have had to face terrible opposition to their work. They have had to fight and struggle against the social, cultural or political interests of their time. The names that have come down to us through history are the ones who have succeeded through their tremendous will-power, courage and spiritedness. It was their enormous moral, spiritual and psychic force – and a lot of hard work – that has brought them success.

Bábá's spiritedness was unshakable. As Ananda Marga was rapidly expanding through its revolutionary ideology, the *adhármika* (unrighteous) forces united to strike a deadly blow to our organization. They stopped at nothing to do harm to the mission, and their malefic activities culminated in a deep-rooted conspiracy against Marga Guru Ánandamúrtijii. He was imprisoned and had to pass through an intolerable ordeal. But nothing could dampen His spirit or His courage, and He remained as resplendent as ever. Undaunted by the malicious propaganda and agonizing experiences in jail, He kept up His fight against all odds. No bestial force could make Him yield or make Him budge from His ideals. There are some incidents which happened in prison that show Bábá's unfathomable courage.

### Lesson for a Liar

Visitors began to throng to the Bankipur jail after Bábá was moved there from the Buxar jail. Initially there were not many restrictions against the visitors. They could come straight to Bábá's cell after getting permission from the jail superintendent. An assistant jailer remained present during the interviews. Sometimes a CID man was present to keep a keep watch on Bábá, but he sat outside the cell.

One day some senior workers came to visit Bábá. He was very weak and remained lying down talking to the visitors. He had just started speaking when two men slid into the room and sat on chairs in the corner of the cell. One of them, we knew, was the assistant jailer, but the other was a complete stranger. Bábá stopped talking and cast a baleful glance at the stranger.

"Who are you?" He asked. The man kept his lips tight as if he had not heard the question.

Bábá would not be put off. He said in a louder voice, "I want to know your identity. Do you follow me?"

The man seemed to have awakened from a trance. "It will not be possible to evade this stubborn man," he must have thought. It was clear to us that he was in a quandary as he could neither keep silent nor could he divulge his identity.

He tried to make light of it saying, "I am an assistant jailer of this jail."

Bábá, I think, knew better. He looked a question at the

assistant jailer seated by the man. The assistant jailer hesitated a moment, then said quietly, "No, he is a CID man."

Bábá flared. He shouted at the man. "Don't you have the courage to speak the truth? You have contaminated the sanctity of this room. Get out, now!"

The CID man was in a pitiable state. We could see that he did not know what to do. The assistant jailer came to his rescue and motioned for him to leave. The CID agent scooted out hastily. He was never seen around the jail again. Later we heard that he had managed to obtain a transfer and had left town forever.

### Pride Gets a Blow

During our stay in jail we had to interact with the jail supervisors and guards on different occasions. They were courteous barring a handful of them. Often Bábá asked us how we were being treated by the jail personnel. He would be satisfied if He heard that we were being treated honourably. If Bábá had heard a positive report about someone, He would be kind and talk politely with him if he visited the cell.

One of the assistant jailers was an officious, vain man. He took every chance to be rude to us without rhyme or reason. This was perhaps due to his ties with the CBI officials.

One day our lawyers had to talk urgently with Bábá about His court case. They came to the jail office with the appropriate application and sought an appointment for an interview. They had not been allowed as on many previous occasions on this or that pretext to meet Bábá, this time they came armed with a court order.

The jail superintendent sent in a brief note about the visitors to Bábá. The visitors could come into the cell if Bábá allowed it. When we learned that that particular assistant jailer would be present during the interview, at the instance of Bábá we wrote a letter to the authorities expressing our unwillingness to allow any outsider hear the interview, since the legal points to be discussed warranted absolute confidentiality. In case the jail authorities insisted on having someone present, he would have to watch from a distance, but not stand within earshot.

The assistant jailer was furious when he came to hear of it. He thought it beneath his status and prestige to remain outside during the interview in the searing summer heat. He demanded to be seated in the cell during the interview. Left with no other option, the jail superintendent sent one request after another to Bábá to allow the assistant jailer to sit in the farthest corner of the cell during the interview.

Bábá remained firm. He said, "How is it possible? We have to consult our lawyers in confidence about our case. It is the law that a person not connected with the case must not be within earshot. So why should there be an exception to it in this matter? We cannot be expected to jeopardize our case because of anyone's prestige or personal status!"

When the jail superintendent realized that Bábá was not going to budge from his decision, he asked the assistant jailer to supervise the interview somehow or other. The assistant jailer made it a prestige issue and refused to comply. Bábá also, supported by His legal rights, would not change His decision. The jail superintendent was in a fix. He would incur the wrath of the court if he sided with his subordinate officer and went to the extent of canceling the interview. Finally he was compelled put in writing an orders to the assistant jailer to supervise the interview according to the conditions laid down by Bábá.

The humiliated assistant jailer had to do an unpleasant

job. His dissatisfaction was written all over his face. But the incident brought about a change in his behaviour. After that we were happy to see that he kept up a good relation with us. Later Bábá said to us, "An antidote should be as strong as, if not stronger than, the poison. Everybody on earth plays with a weakling, but goes down on his knees to worship the mighty."

### The Inspector General Gets Inspected!

In 1972, when Bábá was shifted from the Patna Medical College to the Bankipur Central Jail on medical grounds, the High Court directed the authorities to take appropriate measures so that His imprisonment might not tell upon His health. We heaved a sigh of relief when we heard the order. We thought that Bábá might get a bit of justice, at least to some extent.

I do not know how many appeals we sent during that blistering, torturous summer for an electric fan or air cooler. It never occurred to us that the jailers would disregard the court orders. We wrote to everybody up and down the ladder of authority in the jail system. But the sleeping giant of bureaucracy remained in a coma, or perhaps better to say, a conspiracy.

With this as the backdrop, Bábá played out a drama with Mr. R.K. Srivastava, the inspector general of prisons of Bihar, as the leading star, when he came to visit Bábá's cell.

It was 12 November 1972. The jail authorities were rushing here and there to extend the fitting welcome for the Inspector General of Prisons. The prisoners were made to believe that they were being greatly blessed by the visit of such a great personage to their hovel-like cells. Was an angel arriving from heaven to dole out favours to the lowly? The IG finished inspection of different cells, wards, the infirmary and other units. Now, with the jail superintendent, the jailer and other staff in tow, he strutted into Bábá's cell.

An ominous silence filled the cell. Bábá was lying down on His side facing the wall. There was no welcome here for the IG. Mr. Srivastava was enraged by this rejection. Lying in bed, ill from the heat, Bábá finally broke the silence and started reeling off His grievances one after another. He apprised him of the utter callousness of the jail authorities. He demanded to know what on earth had prevented the IG from acknowledging His letter and why there was as yet no air-cooler provided.

The arrogant Mr. Srivastava was not at all prepared for Bábá's manner of address. Fuming, he threw it all to the wind, and unabashedly shouted, "It was my own verbal order that you won't get any facilities."

Bábá answered back with strong determination, "But your verbal order will not stop me from taking recourse to the law of the courts."

The high-handed IG broke into a fit of frenzy. He scowled at Bábá and said menacingly, "Don't shout at me! You'll pay for this!"

We had information that Mr. Srivastava was in league with the CBI in its conspiracy against Bábá. He was instrumental in meting out many tortures to Bábá. Seeing him threaten Bábá with such violence, the rumours we had heard about his nefarious intentions we now knew to be true. Perhaps Mr. Srivastava was shaken because Bábá's courage had not been affected despite the IG's efforts to indispose Him.

The events which followed proved his threats had not been hollow. In a bid to isolate Bábá, he passed an order that we be shifted to another ward immediately. The medical

privileges we had gotten in the form of medicines were taken away immediately. Then we were all transferred to different jails in Bihar, with nobody left to take care of Bábá in the Bankipur jail.

The IG was setting step by step the stage for a greater drama. It proved we had good reason for our fears. On 12 February 1973, Bábá was poisoned. Bábá dashed the hopes of the conspirators, however. With tremendous inner power, He withstood the poisoning attack, although he could not ward off its side effects. He fell ill, but no one heeded His complaints. He started an historic fast in protest against this and other injustices – a fast that lasted for more than five years and which stirred the world.

Bábá never gave in. What would the history of Ananda Marga have become had He ever compromised His principles or His life? Even the threat of death could not force Him to keep company with *adharma*. Courage, determination and unwavering adherence to His principles formed the tapestry of Bábá's personality.

### Ideology Is More Valuable Than Life

Years of fasting began to eat into the vitals of Bábá. He became emaciated, going from His normal 68 kgs down to 40 kgs. He was reduced to a skeleton, and we were afraid that it would be difficult for Him to survive. The thought that He might be martyred on the altar of *adharma* seemed to rouse the conscience of a great number of political stalwarts like Shrii Atalbihari Bajpayee, Professor Samar Guha and others who came to visit Bábá in His cell. Though Bábá was hardly able to speak, He narrated to them the ill treatment He had been subjected to. The leaders patiently listened to Him and promised to do whatever they could. Before leaving they humbly requested Bábá to break His fast, to save His invaluable life for the sake of humanity.

Bábá said calmly, "I am fasting because of the great ideals I preach and stand for. A single person is not immortal, but one's ideology lives down through the ages. So ideology is always more valuable than a life. I can risk my life, but I will not compromise."

In this way Bábá turned down their requests that He break His fast. His health had dangerously deteriorated, but He neither bent nor showed any sign of breaking. A worker once went to see Him. I was impressed that even enfeebled by fasting and forced to lie constantly in bed, Bábá exhorted him to always live like a tiger, and not like sheep. Many times Bábá told us that we should be as hard as a massive rock, and never as soft as mud, since even an earthworm can dig a hole in mud.

If a *sádhaka* is to stride towards his or her goal, he or she must be established with high morals. The sabre-rattling of evil or amoral people echoes into the void in the long run. They may shout loudly, and their plots appear horrendous, but when they cross swords with a *dhármika*, they are brought down to nothing.

Our goal as well as the means to achieve it was so clearly defined by Bábá. He chose His own course of action and never slackened in His determination. No amount of suffering, no trial of any magnitude, could frighten Him into bargaining with creatures of darkness. Bábá endured the hell of imprisonment and fasted in protest against the evil machinations of those who thrive on the psychic, physical and spiritual exploitation of the people.

CHAPTER 10

# A LESSON I'LL NEVER FORGET

Although every day of Bábá's prison life was significant, it is unfortunate that as hard as I try, some corridors of my memory cannot be illuminated at all. Nevertheless, I can still clearly see in my mind's eye an episode in the Bankipur jail – an incident that is, and will ever be, a fountainhead of inspiration in my life.

It was perhaps May or June 1972, around 8:30 in the evening. Bábá had finished His *sádhaná* and was taking a stroll up and down the deserted paths between the cells. As it was after the general lock-up time, the lanes were calm, empty of prisoners. Before His nightly stroll Bábá used to come to our cell to meet us. He did this every evening and we were usually ready ourselves, so that Bábá might not go back if He saw us absorbed in meditation. But sometimes He happened to come early, and we could hear Him turn away; His footsteps gentle so that our *sádhaná* might not be disturbed. On such occasions, He used to walk alone.

This was such a night. He had been walking for a while when He heard a cry of help coming from a distant ward. Somebody was shouting, "Oh *dádás* (brothers), please come quickly!"

We were very close to many of the convicts, and they looked upon us as their well-wishers. They used to seek our help in their hours of need. We lent a helping hand in whatever way we could. On that night, assuming that we were with Bábá, they were calling to us. They knew that we would respond and come to their help.

But we were not there, and Bábá, realizing their dire need, hastened across the main ward to reach theirs. They were surprised to see Bábá peering closely through the barred door of their cell. They turned shy. Moments were passed by, yet none of them could speak.

Pouring out affection, Bábá said, "Why are you calling your *dádás*? Is there some need? As they are meditating, I have come to you myself."

They began to fumble about saying, "No, Bábá, our need is not so urgent. We'll be obliged if you kindly send the *dádás* to us later."

Bábá said, "They are all doing *sádhaná*. Besides here I am, and I can do what the *dádás* can do. Let me know what you want."

They looked so worried, yet they would not speak out. But Bábá went on insisting that they unburden themselves to Him. At last they told their story. That night just as they were finishing their dinner, the warder had come rushing in with orders to lock up their ward immediately; their open-cell time had run out. The convicts had tried to persuade him to wait just a few minutes more, but the warder was in no mind to compromise. The hungry convicts had swallowed their dry pieces of bread quickly, and the guard had forced them to enter their cells after the role call.

Now there was no provision for water in their cells, and they were extremely thirsty. With parched throats, they had called to the guards who were gossiping away on the lawn a short distance away. The guards had not been moved by the shouts for help of the hapless convicts. Finding no other way, they decided to call us.

Bábá heard their plight and said, "This is not at all a difficult task for me. Wait a little."

Saying this, Bábá came back to His cell. There was an earthen jar of water and a tumbler for His personal use in a corner of the cell. Carrying the jar and tumbler, Bábá re-

turned to the convicts. He poured the water into the tumbler, and extending His hand, held it out to them. But the convicts had qualms of their own; they said that under no circumstances would they allow Bábá serve them.

Bábá stubbornly continued to persuade them. He cajoled them by saying that, contrary to their apprehensions, He would get pleasure if He could serve them. At last, Bábá gave them all water, one by one. Their thirst quenched, they gazed at Bábá, not knowing how to express their gratitude. Then Bábá went back with the jar and tumbler. He put them back and continued His walk along the empty stretch.

At the time we had known nothing of all this. The door of our cell had in the meantime been opened by the guard, and we had joined Bábá. He had talked to us as usual and had not said anything about what had happened earlier. At nine o'clock we finished our walk and were locked up for the night.

The next morning Bábá was doing *sádhaná*. Those convicts came to us, their faces gleaming with gladness while we were preparing ourselves to attend on Him. They told us of the previous night's episode and were stunned that Bábá had made no mention about the matter to us.

Later, I was thinking of Bábá's divine gesture though I was supposed to be doing my *sádhaná* instead with one-pointed attention. Even in the mini-society of the jail the prevailing social structure separates the privileged from the poorer classes. Usually the political prisoners would disdain to stop for, let alone help, lower class convicts. Bábá threw to the winds all such jail "etiquette" and risked His own security to help those in need.

Despite their repeated requests to call us instead, and their unwillingness to accept His service, Bábá had troubled Himself to come to their aid. Though it was beyond their understanding, the lesson was clear to me. He would never disturb us in *sádhaná*. And what if He did? Would there have been any downfall for us, if He, the source of our knowledge and the goal of our *sádhaná*, had called us from meditation? Would we have suffered greatly if, at His instance, we had left our *sádhaná* halfway and rushed to the aid of those thirsty convicts? The answer is: obviously not; yet He would never do it. He taught us by example that *sádhaná* should be performed with utmost sincerity, and He wanted everybody to be as sincere as He was.

Who knows how much Bábá did for others, never mentioning it to anyone? It was only after someone would come and tell us about some incident that we would find out what He did for others in the course of everyday life. Though we had walked with Him that night for a long time He had never said anything about what had happened.

We were waiting impatiently for Bábá to finish morning *sádhaná*. We hastened to Him the moment He got up. I could not subdue my curiosity any longer. I said to Him, "Last night you went to the convicts' ward; you came back to your cell to get the jar and tumbler; you then served them water. All this, yet you did not give us even a hint of it!"

Bábá said, "Did I do anything that merits propaganda? It was part of my duty, so I did it. Why should there be any proclamation for performing one's duty?"

I had nothing to say to this so I kept silent. An inexpressible feeling flooded my mind. It would be impossible to measure the broadness and softness of His heart. I looked at Him in wonder and thought how profound His sense of duty was. I discovered anew that He could only be compared to Himself; there was no one like Him.

CHAPTER 11

### ANTARYÁMII BÁBÁ

The Indian sages named eight occult powers that one can achieve through one-pointed *sádhaná*. These powers are: *animá*, to become small (small enough to enter any physical particle or any crevice of another's mind); *laghimá*, to become light (a light body can fly through air, a light mind can study the minds of others); *mahimá*, to become large (an expanded mind is omniscient, and feels love for the universe); *iishitva*, to control (this supreme control may be used to guide others' minds); *vashitva*, to psychic dominate others; *prápti*, to obtain any desired object; *prakámya*, to materialize the desired outcome of events; and *antaryámitva*, to know the inner thought-wave of anyone. These supernatural powers are called *vibhúti*. (Note that though some of the powers may be used for similar ends, the ends are achieved by different methods.) People may desire these eight powers (*aśta siddhi*), but Bábá warned us against giving in to any temptation to get these powers. To yearn for these powers, the guru has said, is utter foolishness.

The first man known in history to possess all eight powers was Sadáshiva, who lived about seven thousand years ago. The people called Him with great respect "Vibhútinátha" or "Vibhútibhuśańa" (Lord of the *Vibhútis*). Sadáshiva, popularly known as Shiva, used to cover his body with ashes because he wanted to impress upon ordinary people, who have an inherent lust for power and a natural attraction for the supernatural, that these powers have no more value than a handful of ashes.

According to the principles of grammar the word *vibhúti* means "ashes". So even in this sense Shiva is rightly called Vibhútinátha or Vibhútibhuśańa, because for enlightened beings those powers are nothing more than ashes, they never bother about them.

#### Antaryámii – Knower of the Inner Heart

Many people used to come to see Bábá in Jamalpur. He would sit in *darshan* (audience) regularly and give philosophical discourses. He would meet with His devotees privately, patiently hear all their problems and give solutions. There is no doubt that Bábá was an *antaryámii*. Anyone came in contact with Him had at least one tale to tell of their experience with His *antaryámitva*. Knowledge of the past, present and future was at His command.

Whenever it was necessary, Bábá could paint a picture of a person's past, telling everything he or she had done. He could tell about the coming events of a person and inspire them towards a bright and blissful future. People felt that not only did Bábá know every particular fact of each person's life, but even the minutest thoughts of their innermost hearts would be clearly reflected in His compassionate consciousness. There was no use in trying to deceive Him. He was present in the minds of all.

#### The University Student

When Bábá was staying at the railway quarters in Jamalpur He used to come to the *jágrti* and give *darshan* everyday, in the mornings as well as the evenings. There was always a crowd of devotees; many came from far-off places. Bábá would eloquently explain many spiritual topics. During *darshan* itself He would sometimes suggest

solutions to different people's domestic and personal problems, or correct an erring disciple. Bábá saw into their lives.

Some time in the sixties, a university student came to the *jágrti* one day. He was a good boy, but he smoked cigarettes. He was a regular smoker though he was fully aware that Gurudeva knew of his habit.

He had an idea. "Bábá may know everything while He is awake," he thought, "but how could He know things when He sleeps?" So, he decided to smoke only in the night when he figured Bábá was asleep. The student had another idea as well. He thought that Bábá must have a big library in His home. Otherwise how does He give such learned and wise discourses every day?

One day after Bábá had finished His discourse, He asked that Margi student to stand. The boy obeyed and wondered what Bábá wanted.

Bábá asked what was there in the side pocket of the boy's trousers. Now the poor fellow was in a very sticky position. He tried to evade the question by muttering inarticulately.

Bábá then said: "Well, why not just bring the contents of your pocket out and place them down in front of everybody?" At this the boy made his last attempt to defend himself.

Bábá addressed a devotee sitting next to the student, "Look, the boy is possibly feeling shy to show his belongings in public. Please help him." Now he was done for. Finding no escape, he most reluctantly emptied his pockets, and along with some odds and ends was a packet of cigarettes and a matchbox. His face crimson, it was beyond his imagination that he would ever be caught.

With a smile, Bábá said, "You know, there's a boy who thinks that Bábá loses awareness when He sleeps and does not know anything. So that boy smokes to his heart's content during the night when he thinks Bábá is in deep sleep. Can anyone say who that boy is?"

At this the Margi hung his head and replied, "Me, Bábá."

Later that evening Bábá again showed that student that He knew everything in his heart.

Bábá was on His evening field walk with some devotees. The student joined in for the return walk. Bábá was engaged in conversation with the devotees. When they reached home, He took leave of others at the gate, but He told the Margi to come inside the house. Bábá casually showed him the rooms, one after another.

Then He said, "Well, have you seen all the rooms of my house, my little boy?" The boy, oblivious to the intention, nodded his head. Bábá added, "The Bábá you know talks on so many subjects. Can you tell me which book or library is the source of His knowledge?"

The boy was struck dumb. The fact is that Bábá had no library or book in His house. He didn't need them. His knowledge was not gained from books, but from an all-knowing intuition. Knowledge was spontaneously revealed to His consciousness. All his doubts were gone, the Margi realized that Bábá not only had extraordinary knowledge on various subjects, He had special knowledge of himself as well.

#### Two Lakh Rupees

After completing His *sádhaná* one evening, Bábá had settled in His room when, without warning, a man tiptoed in and kneeled in obeisance on the floor. In his hand was an expensive bag which he placed near Bábá's feet. Smugly,

he said, “Bábá, here is one lakh rupees (Rs. 100,000), please accept it.”

Bábá’s eyes became inscrutable. He said, “Well, that is very good, but where is the other lakh?”

The man was baffled. He shivered as fear trickled down his spine. “How could Bábá know of the other lakh?” he wondered to himself. The matter was a secret known only to him. No one else was expected to know it. Maybe...

Meanwhile Bábá grabbed the bag and threw it out of the window. The man jumped up, rushed outside to retrieve the bag and ran away. Instantly Bábá closed the door and settled to His own work.

Bábá told us this story sometime later. He explained His actions by telling the story of this man’s past, and what the future holds in store for him. It seems that by various unscrupulous means and committing heinous crimes throughout his life, that man amassed Rs. 200,000. He came to Bábá thinking that if he could appease Bábá with the money then his sins would be nullified. It was not to be.

Bábá said, “He is a great sinner and the complete retribution of his sins is about to begin. He has sunk to the very depth of vices. His wife also is a characterless woman. He has kept that money hidden, but very soon she will come to know of it and will steal the entire amount. Then she will elope with another man. That man will then be completely heartbroken and wrecked and will pass his days as a hapless destitute. Moreover his only child – a son – will die soon after that, and through an accident one of his legs will become lame, pushing him to the limit of endurance. His last days will be unbearable. Crushed under the weight of his painful life, he will then chant the name of Rám (God), crying ‘O Rám! O Rám! What have I done?’ Then the day will come when, in pathetic conditions, his life’s drama will end. Only then will the entire accumulation of his *saḿskáras* from his vices and sins be exhausted.”

#### Need You Ask?

The mission was still small in the early sixties. Bábá would spend a lot of time with His disciples, teaching and talking to them about their personal lives and domestic affairs. Bábá would greet them and ask them about their families’ well-being. He would ask about their sons and daughters – what they were doing, how they were, etc. The devotees and their families were overwhelmed to feel that Bábá was taking so much care of them.

An older, pious devotee came to Jamalpur to meet Bábá. He was a highly placed official in the Bihar State government. He was what you would call a dry honest person. Bábá had great affection for him. Whenever he came, Bábá conversed with him very sweetly.

One day he thought, “Bábá is supposed to be omniscient. Why does He bother to ask about how they are if He can see into other people’s minds?” The question haunted him. He expressed his doubts to other devotees without getting a satisfactory reply.

After a little while Bábá arrived. He exchanged *namaskárs* with everyone and affectionately asked so many questions about each person’s life. He listened to them and suggested different solutions for problems to some devotees. Everybody was happy. Bábá walked on and did not say a single word to nor look at that gentleman. The Margii silently sat all alone, crestfallen. After the *darshan* was over Bábá left for His residence.

The whole day the gentleman was in mental turmoil. He was extremely disturbed, feeling not a little self-reproach.

“What a fool I am,” he thought. “How I doubted Bábá’s omniscience!” He realized why Bábá had not paid any attention to him. He cursed himself thoroughly.

In the evening, after office hours, Bábá came to the *jágrti*. As soon as Bábá went back to his room, the man asked permission to speak with Him. He ran to Bábá and threw himself at His feet. Remorsefully he said, “Bábá, I have committed a great mistake, and You have stopped talking with me. You did not even ask how I am.”

Bábá replied, “Why? What is there to ask about? Don’t I know all about you?” The gentleman condemned himself for his lack of faith.

Affectionately Bábá said, “Look, my role is not just as a spiritual teacher. I also teach social behaviour. According to social norms, it is expected whenever a civilized person sees another for the first time in the day, he greets him or her with good wishes. Not to do so will be impolite. Whether or not I know anything about anybody is not the measuring stick of my social etiquette. No one can learn from that. People learn from how I behave and from what I do in the daily business of life.”

When I heard this story it struck me that Bábá’s every word and action is meaningful and educative and not to be taken lightly.

### Urgent Wire from Jamalpur

A touching event occurred in June 1972. That day we had attended the hearing of our case in the Patna court. After the hearing ended, our lawyers conveyed us a message from our Patna headquarters. We returned to the jail a little before evening. Usually when we came back from court, we visited Bábá to apprise Him of the court’s proceedings. We did the same on this day too. We narrated all that happened: the statements of the witnesses, our lawyers’ the cross-examinations and so on. Bábá was pleased to hear about it. But the telegram posed a problem for us. What were we to say? He was imprisoned, ill and bedridden. How could we tell this upsetting news to Bábá? But we certainly could not suppress it. So when we finished the court news, we hesitated.

Bábá asked, “Well, anything more you want to say?”

I replied in a low voice, “Yes Bábá, from Jamalpur there is –”

# STRONG AS THUNDER – TENDER AS A FLOWER BUD

Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti was omniscient, a knower of minds. Past, present and future were at His fingertips. Tantric scriptures have defined *sadguru* as one who can be as strict in inflicting strong punishment on an erring disciple as He is affectionate to a good disciple. Bábá, in fact, was as strong as thunder in dealing with evil or immoral actions on the one hand, and as tender as a flower bud in dealing with righteous or moral actions on the other.

Actually, I never felt Bábá's anger was in any sense like an ordinary person's. Usually if someone loses his temper, his blood pressure rises, his face turns red and he loses all mental equilibrium. But Bábá was always in control of Himself. He would show anger and displeasure to make us realize our faults and goad us on towards inner and outer perfection. I always felt that He was playing a role with His anger for us because, in the next moment, He could be light-hearted again – laughing and making us laugh.

It was useless to try to evade Bábá's omniscient surveillance. When He worked in government service, it was impossible for any of His colleagues to conceal their misdeeds. And in our organization, anyone attempting to avoid Bábá's scrutiny was always caught red-handed. If any worker of the organization was guilty of misconduct, His situation would be most pathetic. Their utter discomfiture would defy all description. If a disciple did anything good, however humble it might be, it would evoke Bábá's unbounded love. Bábá would praise him as "a gem of a boy" or her as "a gem of a girl". He would inspire them beyond their expectations.

Rabindranath Tagore once wrote, "He who loves is the only one who has the right to chastise." Bábá synthesized perfectly these two opposing qualities. Very often He would say, "If your love for someone is 100%, see that your chastisement does not exceed 75%. The degree of control should never go beyond the degree of love."

It is quite natural for people to occasionally commit mistakes in their work. But Bábá would not tolerate any mistake, and His whole demeanour would quickly assume a furious appearance. Anyone who has no personal experience of seeing this fury will never be able to guess how terrifying it could be. It would seem that flames of fire were shooting out of His eyes. Who could look Him in the eye! And His rebukes were so harsh and piercing! There was no one who could withstand those stormy reprimands. Our condition was so pitiable in those moments we would wish the earth to swallow us up. When Bábá was in a stern mood, censuring us for a long time, most of us wanted to leave the room on some pretext or other; we wanted to escape from His presence. If we did manage it, we would sigh, "Oh thank God!" and that would be the end of the trouble for the time being.

Truly, we were not so unnerved by His anger, even though it blew over us like a devastating storm. We knew that soothing rainfall was sure to follow. The severity of the prolonged reproofs and condemnation we had to face during our reporting sessions, pierced through our minds like arrows and made us completely heartbroken. We would be then hopelessly dejected. But when the reporting was over, He would change completely. He would call us and shower loving caresses and sweet, calming words on us. This love, this affection, was so sublime and touching that all the

humiliation, dejection and agonies we were experiencing a moment before were instantly gone. We were again back to our usual selves. Bábá would then say tenderly, "You know, my child, my position is like that of the judge. From this position, everything good or bad should be judged impartially on the scales of justice. There is no scope for thinking otherwise. That is indeed a hard responsibility. Moreover, you know, a little fear is also necessary. Only then can a man move on the right path. As Tulsidasji used to say, 'No love without fear.'"

I was talking with Bábá one day. He said, "You see, no matter how high a position a person attains, he or she will always need a strict guardian to answer to for his or her deeds—good or bad—and who will give him or her proper guidance in life's journey. That guardian will also be a perennial source of inspiration. You see, I play that role. That is why I have a responsibility to be very strict in my discipline and duties. But it is not my real nature. My responsibility compels me, against my wish, to be harsh with you. Infinite affection for all is my real nature, not my anger, fury, and harshness. What you see is nothing but camouflage to an inner ocean of love and affection for all."

### The Coconut and the Wood-Apple

He would often mention the example of the coconut and wood-apple (*bel* fruit) to illustrate this point. "You all are familiar with coconuts and wood-apples. These two are very good, tasty fruits. The inner portion of both is extremely sweet, soft and delicious; the coconut gives a sweet drink, also. But, you see, both have very rough, hard, impenetrable shells. It would otherwise be impossible to protect their inner softness from the onslaught of birds and animals. So too, a perfect person, endowed with all good qualities, needs to have a rough exterior, or else there will be no dearth of people to exploit his goodness. Exploiting his honesty and sincerity, people will put him into great trouble and will try to draw personal gain from him by unjust means. Ordinary people in general have a tendency to be like this. There is a proverb in Hindi, *Sídhá muh kuttá cháte*, which means 'A simple man is an easy object of humiliation'. A simple, honest man is bound to stumble at every step. Others will make a fool of him and fulfil their self-interests."

This certainly would not have been possible from reading books; He could have gotten this ability only through intuition. It did not depend on any external means.

As the president of Ananda Marga, He kept a constant vigil over every member of the organization and assessed their performance. At the same time, He was the spiritual master and would watch over the spiritual aspect of everyone's life. His all-knowing power was clearly evident. Whether in organizational affairs or personal duty, no slackness would be excused by Bábá. Bábá could rain a shower of condemnation on an offender.

He had His own special vocabulary He used in His scoldings such as *Yamer aruci* ("a person whom even Death shuns") and *pińd́igelá bhút* ("a ghost whose only desire is to eat the funerary offerings"). When Bábá saw that the barrage of words had taken effect, He would return to His natural tender self. This change of mood from extreme severity to sweet affection was instantaneous. He was transformed into one belonging to another world. It was impossible to believe that this embodiment of bliss (*Ánandamúrti*) was, a moment back, a blazing fire.

### The Guru and the Disciple

Tantric literature speaks of three distinct classes of gurus. There is in the first place the *adhama guru*, that is, the most inferior type. He or she gives advice and lectures in high-sounding words, but does not bother about whether the disciples follow his or her instructions or are benefited at all.

The second type is one who gives advice as the first type does, but does not stop there. This guru keeps a proper watch over the disciples' activities, and gives inspiration to follow all the advice. The rest is left in the hands of the disciple. He or she may or may not follow the advice given. This *madhyama guru* finishes his or her duty at this stage.

The third type of guru, *uttama guru*, the most ideal guru, is one who gives advice, provides necessary inspiration, and takes complete responsibility to guide the disciple through the path. In other words, an ideal preceptor does not stop at merely preaching. He or she takes the responsibility to bring the disciple to the goal. It is his or her sacred duty to take the disciple by the hand and help him or her progress step by step.

Once Bábá said, "Do you know how the guru-disciple relationship is? The guru and the disciple are holding each other's hands." He grasped the wrist of a worker standing nearby. Bábá continued, "Just in this way. Let us assume that, for any reason, maybe weakness or circumstantial pressure, the disciple loses his grip, the guru's hand will keep him steady even then. The guru never slackens his grip, and the flow of his mercy never dries up. But if the disciple loses his hold and starts blaming his guru, denouncing his spiritual precepts, the guru is obliged to withhold his support. Such a position is extremely painful – suicidal – for the disciple.

"That is why, when a student fails in his or her school examination, I accuse the teacher, not the student. It is true that not all students can be expected to stand first in the class, yet is equally true that for failure, responsibility lies more with the teacher. He or she must have been negligent in performing his or her duties for the student to fail."

CHAPTER 13

# SUPER-MASTER IN THE ART OF EXPRESSION

In everything a person does, in every manifestation of one's personality, there is an art. The art of speech is no exception. One who can acquire mastery over it is sure to be successful in life. Bábá said, “If one knows the art of speaking, one can say it in a thousand ways;” and again, “Although the job of a speaker is to speak, it is no easy job to speak something worthwhile.”

Words can be used in different ways according to time, place and person. The right word at the right time may create an altogether different effect in a particular situation. Bábá cited an example: “Assume that you are having a discussion with a gentleman about a particular subject. During the conversation he makes a statement which you think is not true. Rather, you are sure that he is making a false statement. So you bluntly say to him, ‘Well sir, what you say is a lie.’ However untrue his statement be, the man will surely be offended at your direct attack. Moreover, if he is short-tempered, it will cause an additional problem. At once he will flare up; he may even curse you.”

“But if you say instead,” Bábá continued, “ ‘Yes, what you say is undoubtedly very convincing, but it appears that you have been unfortunately misinformed by someone.’ It will have the same meaning; indirectly you are calling him a liar, but your expression of the same truth is now so polite and dignified that the man will hear your point and will be ashamed for telling a lie. He may even beg your pardon.”

I had a chance to make use of this lesson with a certain gentleman I knew well. He had many good qualities, but just as the Lord did not create anyone with vices only, neither did He create anyone 100 percent virtuous. People are a mixture of good and bad. This man had one big fault: he became furious at the smallest thing and unhesitatingly abused the offender. And if anybody said anything about his temper, the situation would go totally beyond control and become dangerous. His temper would soar high into the stratosphere.

One day something happened that set this man's temper off. I was summoned to mediate the crisis. I first listened to all the details of what had happened. Initially, on the basis of what I understood, I supported him. I even mildly reprimanded those who had provoked him. The man was pleased at this, and within moments regained his normal disposition. This was the opportune moment to make him conscious of his bad temper.

Calmly and slowly I started, “Well brother, I am simply amazed. *Parama Puruśa* has endowed you with so many rare qualities: your wisdom and intellect has no comparison; there is a beautiful synthesis of *bhakti* and karma in you; your extraordinary honesty and courage are examples for others. But unfortunately, now and then you lose control of yourself. You become red with an anger which, I am afraid to say, is not always justified. This time your behaviour was rude and rough, and that puts you in error in spite of your innumerable good qualities. If, however, you were a little alert about it, your unique personality would be a real pride to all.

“Moreover there is a saying of Bábá's: ‘A perfect *sádhaka* is one who can control his or her passions even when provoked.’ Bábá's invaluable words should be kept in mind, don't you think?”

When I finished my little talk he became extremely re-

morseful and was sorry for his discourteous behaviour. Not only that, he assured me that in the future he would try to keep his temper under control and behave in a proper manner.

### Gopál Bháń's Wit

One day Bábá was talking about Gopál Bháń, a famous court jester of King Krśńacandra of Krishnanagar. Gopál Bháń was an unrivalled speaker with intelligence and a ready wit. To illustrate the point Bábá narrated a humorous story:

Once the nawab of Murshidabad, along with his retinue, arrived at the court of King Krśńacandra. He was given a royal welcome by the maharaja with all pomp and splendour. The nawab was very pleased with the king's hospitality and finally took leave of him and set out for his own capital. Along the way he passed a farm. A huge, healthy bull was grazing in the field. The nawab could not resist this temptation and asked his attendants to catch it. He would take it with him to his capital. 'I'll prepare a feast with it,' he thought. The very thought made his mouth water.

The people of Krishnanagar were in an uproar when they found out about the bull. The scholars, the priests and all the religious folk were up in arms at the sacrilege committed. Frantic appeals were made to save the religion of the Hindus, which forbids the slaughtering of cows and eating of beef. Hinduism was at stake! The nawab had unscrupulously stolen a bull that was, in fact, dedicated to God, under the nose of the king. “He will make a feast of it! Oh God, we are lost! Our religion is defiled!” So the entire population of pandits, Brahmans and Vaishnavas rallied to the maharaja's court.

The maharaja was in a bind. The person was no less than the nawab himself. Who would dare risk his life to get back the bull from the nawab? But the religious sentiments of the people had been seriously affronted. The bull must be brought home by any means.

The maharaja knew that only Gopál Bháń could come to the rescue. He was sure to manage a satisfactory solution. He sent for Gopál Bháń, who hastened at once to the king's chambers. After hearing the whole story, he reassured the maharaja and started immediately for Murshidabad, the capital of the nawab. He arrived just in time. It was not difficult for him to get permission to see the nawab because he was famous as the court jester of the maharaja. The nawab was curious to know why Gopál had come to visit so soon after his own recent departure from Krishnanagar.

After offering due respects and honour to the nawab, Gopál Bháń conveyed the king's gratitude to him. The nawab was rather puzzled. What had he done for the maharaja that prompted him to send Gopál Bháń rushing across the country to the nawab?

Gopál was extremely humble; submissively he said, “Nawab Sahib, you are really great. You have done a wonderful service for the people of Krishnanagar. That is why the maharaja has sent me to you to express his heartfelt gratitude on behalf of the people of Krishnanagar.”

“Is that so, Gopál?” exclaimed the nawab, “I don't understand what you are talking about. What is it the maharaja and the people of Krishnanagar are so grateful for?”

“Nawab Sahib,” Gopál said, “the bull you have so kindly brought with you was a big nuisance to our people. Whenever anyone had to ease himself somewhere in the open, that bull would invariably show up, chase him away and eat the excrement up clean. What a complete annoyance for every-

one! From now on, our people will sigh in relief every time and will be able to ease themselves in peace.”

“What do you say, Gopál? Alas! the bull eats human excrement! And I was going to eat it! Fie on me! Even the thought of it makes me sick! You take this bull back right now, Gopál. Don’t delay!”

“As you please, Your Excellency,” Gopál Bháń said in apparent dismay, “your order shall be obeyed.” Gopál thus fulfilled his mission and returned home joyfully.

### Right Answers to Questions

Once in the seventies, Bábá was traveling by air, perhaps from Delhi to Ranchi. At some point during the flight Bábá was discussing philology. In order to make His point clear and intelligible, He frequently translated sentences into different languages of the world. He pointed out and explained in detail the grammatical and pronunciation defects inherent in different languages, what should actually be their correct grammatical forms and how they should be pronounced correctly according to the science of phonetics. Overhearing Bábá’s conversation and impressed by Bábá’s knowledge, a gentleman sitting just beside Him could not restrain his curiosity.

He turned to Bábá and asked, “Excuse me, how many languages do you know?”

Bábá replied with a smile, “By your grace, I know all the languages of the world, my son.”

This reply is certainly instructive. Had Bábá replied that He knew nothing, it would have appeared modest but would have been false, for in fact, He knew all languages. On the other hand, if He said, “I know all languages,” that would, of course, have been true, but would have smacked of egotism. Bábá’s reply was such that steered clear of these two unpleasant alternatives. The gentleman was simply charmed by Bábá’s sweet way of expressing Himself.

Bábá was a super-master in the art of talking – a living guide. Our relationship with Bábá was so intimate that whenever we saw Him in private, He affectionately called us close to Him. “Come, come, sit near me. Tell me how things are going on,” He would say in a sweet voice.

Bábá’s relationship with each of us was personal and intimate. Breaking all artificial barriers of etiquette, He addressed us in informal language. He also wanted that we should not use formal language with Him; we addressed Him as *tumi* (informal form of “you”). Bábá’s intimacy would make each of us feel we were His one and only child. He would say with simple tenderness, “You know, my son, my relationship with you is so deep and close that I feel happier if you address me with *tumi* rather than *ápni*.”

“When I talk to you in private,” Bábá added, “I am a different person altogether in another world. But in the presence of others, everyone should be treated with due respect. Naturally I then have to talk with a little reservation. This is called social courtesy. It is incumbent on any mannered person to obey the rules of social courtesy in every sphere of life.”

It is this close, personal relationship with one’s *Iśta* that inspires a devotee to sing on a bright morning, “O my Friend, lead me onwards” (*Bandhu he niye calo*). When one feels so near to the Supreme Father, when one feels He is one’s closest friend and parent, one aspires to merge into that infinite love. Bábá, as a master in the art of expression, brought that closeness to everyone.

# DHARMA SAMIIKŚA – A UNIQUE EXPERIENCE

Nineteen eighty-one was a memorable year for Ananda Margis. It will surely go down in history as the year we were blessed with *Dharma Samiikśa*. In June and July, Marga Guru reviewed one by one the past and present actions of a horde of disciples and devotees. That was an experience that this planet had never seen before and I doubt will ever see again. Ác. Vijayánanda Avt. and I had the unique privilege of being present throughout the entire period of the review. My position as general secretary allowed me to come very close to Bábá at this time.

Initially, Bábá had given many *sádhakas* Personal Contact. They had had the opportunity of coming very close to Bábá. The main purpose of Personal Contact was to establish a spiritual rapport between the guru and the disciple. It was not merely to have a mechanical or formal relationship. The all-knowing Bábá drew His beloved disciples close to Him in PC. He unfolded their past – their mistakes and so on – which no one but the disciple knew. Bábá then washed the slate of their past clean and helped them progress towards their goal.

*Dharma Samiikśa* was somewhat different. Bábá would analyse the physical, psychic and spiritual aspects of those who came before Him. All the causes of the obstacles in a *sádhaka’s* spiritual life were detected. Bábá would proceed to root out those causes the way you would extract a thorn from your finger with another thorn.

It did not take more than a few minutes for Bábá to perceive the psychic, spiritual and physical needs of the *sádhaka*. He would then review in detail all his or her faults and give advice how to rectify those faults, both mentally and physically. Afterwards, the *sádhaka* could move on his or her spiritual path with pure heart and renewed determination.

Bábá would adopt a particular mood in these situations. He called it *vyáśásana* – the seat of judgement. He kept strict vigilance over what each devotee did: whether they followed Sixteen Points, whether they did social service and in what ways they tried to improve their character. And whenever any lapse on the part of an aspirant was detected, Bábá, in *vyáśásana*, would find the mental, physical, spiritual factors responsible for the lapses. It was something like an expert jeweller testing the purity of gold. Not even the slightest impurity escaped detection.

As soon as the news that every Margi was to come to India for *Dharma Samiikśa*, our small Jodhpur Park office in Calcutta was stormed by devotees from all over the world. Day after day a long queue formed; every Margi waited in the sweltering summer heat for his or her turn. Bábá would arrive from His residence in Lake Gardens at ten in the morning and again at five in the afternoon. *Dharma Samiikśa* would then begin and go on for hours together.

My duty was to take a small group of five to seven people at a time into Bábá’s room and have them stand one by one in front of Him. Dádá Vijayananda and I were there to assist Bábá whenever He desired. Bábá would first ask each one his or her name and address, and the name of his or her *ácárya* then go on to other questions or to His own observations.

Everyone’s review revealed an amazing history. I remember one that touched me in particular. A young man about twenty-one years old came for his review. He looked quiet,

smart and educated. After asking his name Bábá wanted to know what work he did for the mission. Modestly, the young man said that he was working in an Ananda Marga primary school and children’s home. His duty was to look after the four boys of the home.

After listening to what he had to say, Bábá stared for a time straight at him. The analysis was thorough. The physical body and its organs, the subconscious and deeper layers of the mind – all were carefully looked at. An X-ray, so to speak, would be taken of the person’s past, present and future. Then Bábá would present a kaleidoscopic picture of his or her entire life.

Bábá turned to me and said, “You see, Sarveshvaránanda, on the whole this little boy is a good chap; he practises a little meditation, tries to follow the rules of conduct, does some social work, but after all, I am sitting in my *vyásásana*. I have to keep watch over each disciple’s life and weigh impartially both the good and the bad. Then I will decide what steps have to be taken.”

Bábá looked back to the young man, “So, my little boy, you are in charge of the children’s home, isn’t it? You teach some classes in the school. And if visitors arrive, you see they are well taken care of, I presume.”

“Yes, that’s so, Bábá,” the young man replied confidently.

“Well, do you remember about a fortnight back when some visitors came to the children’s home in the morning?”

“Yes, Bábá,” came the prompt reply.

The young man nodded his head happy that Bábá knew of the visit. “What kind of sweets were they, my little boy? Was it *rasagollá*? And some vegetable cutlets and *siṅgáŕá*, I think.”

“Exactly, Bábá.”

“You sent one of the little boys to the market to buy the food?”

“Yes, Bábá.”

“You joined the guests while they were eating?”

“Yes, Bábá.”

### The Strong Guardian

People are a mixture of good and bad. No one is wholly evil nor wholly virtuous. At some time or other, everybody commits mistakes and errors, big and small. For this reason a person needs on his or her life’s journey a strong guide who will correct his or her mistakes and inspire him or her to the right path. Bábá used to say that a person, however great in wisdom or experience, needs an ever-watchful, strong guardian, even up to the moment of his death. As far as we are concerned, Bábá was – and is – our guardian. I have realized that at every step of my life.

At times, Bábá would warn us, saying, “You should know that the subtle vision of *Parama Puruśa* can reach even where the sun’s rays fail to enter.” If any wholetimer or Margi, in any part of the world, did anything wrong, it was immediately known to Him, reflected in His mental mirror. At our monthly reporting sessions He often exposed someone’s mistakes, giving a flawless, comprehensive description of them in front of the other workers. We felt from the centre of our hearts that He was watching over us with love but with a vigilant eye.

I remember an incident that occurred during *Dharma Samiikśa*. A gentleman, around fifty years old, was standing in line behind four or five others. He was from Uttar Pradesh. Although he was not very handsome, he was immaculately dressed. He carried himself with an air of superiority, and a demeanour full of pride. He saw that so many devotees were going into Bábá’s room and making open, unreserved confessions to Bábá, who in turn rebuked them and in some cases punished them also!

His turn came very soon, however. Bábá in His usual polite mood, asked his name, where he came from, etc. To His question about academic qualifications, the devotee proudly replied, “I have an MA degree in philosophy.” I could see by his way of speaking that he must have thought to himself that others were fools to make such openhearted confessions in public, and gladly accepting punishment on top of it. And that no, he was not going to expose himself like that. He would not break; he was a very tough nut to crack.

“Bravo! Well done,” was Bábá’s appreciative response. Then turning to me with a smile, He said, “Well, GS Dádá, you see, he seems to be a great scholar and looks very intelligent, too. What do you say?”

### Ask Only for Steadfast Devotion

It was during the early sixties. On this particular day, one senior devotee joined in Bábá’s field walk to the outskirts of Jamalpur. During the walk, Bábá was talking, occasionally drawing his attention to some point of the discussion.

Suddenly Bábá stopped and turned to him, “You see, I never leave myself indebted to anyone. If anybody does ‘this little’ for me, I do ‘this much’ for him in return.”

Bábá gestured showing ‘this little’ with His palms close together, and ‘this much’ with His arms spread wide. It is said that the word *krpá* (“grace”) means to receive something in return for doing something for the Lord. The root verb *kr* means “to do” and *pá* means “to get”. The conjoined meaning is “do for Him and get in return”. Bábá wanted to impress on the devotee the point that if anybody does anything for Him, He returns it a hundred times. It is most unfortunate that people do not generally follow this path of substantial gain.

Human nature is such that people try to get as much as they can and do nothing in return. Even though *Parama Puruśa* has created everything for the good of human beings, made ample provision for their needs, people are never satisfied. Their eternal cry is “Give me more! Give me more!” They run from one place to another like madmen – in search of what, they do not know. Thus the beauty of the Lord’s spontaneous gift is lost to them. Their unsatisfied desire for more and more leads them into a dark abyss of sorrows.

Once we were talking with Bábá on this subject. Bábá was in a blissful mood. He started, “You know, *Parama Puruśa* knows the ins and outs of each and every person. Of only humans? No, the past, present and future of all beings is known to the Supreme Being. Who is there, who realizes the inner needs of people better than *Parama Puruśa*? He is there to fulfil all material needs, whenever it is necessary. There is no need to ask Him for anything. If anything at all, one should ask Him for steadfast devotion and nothing else. ‘You are the only object of my desire, the only object of my

knowledge. You are the operator, I am Your instrument.’ This is the best attitude one can adopt.”

“Yet,” Bábá continued, “if anyone earnestly asks for any material boon, I may grant that prayer. But you know, just as I give with one hand, I take with the other. So if I grant a prayer for material gain, it is not of much use. Rather it works like flood water, washing out the water in a pond.” Meaning that whatever positive thing is there will be lost, washed away in the tide of his or her greed.

Then Bábá related the tale of the monkey’s paw.

Once a certain disciple had gone to his guru’s *áshram* and served him for many years. Besides the regular daily worship and prayer, he took great pains to do whatever his guru desired. Naturally, the guru was highly pleased with him. The disciple’s sincerity, devotion and service made a deep impression on him.

One day the guru called him. “Look here, you have been in this *áshram* for a long time, leaving your home, your wife and children behind. I am pleased with your work here. Now you had better go back to look after your family and devote yourself in prayer and meditation at home. As far as possible, do some service work also.” With these words, the guru placed his hand on the disciple’s head and blessed him, “God will always be with you. May you be prosperous.”

The disciple, however, was not satisfied. His inner desire had not been fulfilled. The guru was quick to feel the suppressed discontent of the disciple. He noticed a black spot in the disciple’s mind and a gloomy darkness on his face. It seemed he was suffering some sort of mental agony.

“Does he have some other desire?” thought the guru. Then a clear picture of the disciple’s desire arose in the guru’s mind. Looking at him, he shouted, “What is that you want?”

The disciple was stunned at the sudden outburst of the guru, but he did not change his mind. He had expected that if the guru was pleased with his service, he would receive a substantial boon from him. How could he be satisfied with a mere abstract blessing? What would others say when he returned home? He must have something extraordinary, something special, to show. So he gathered up courage and said, “Gurudeva, you have not favoured me with any supernatural power!”

The guru was in a rage. He spoke ominously, “So is that it, fool! You want supernatural powers? You are not to be satisfied with what I have willingly given on my own?” The guru reached for something and handed it to the disciple. “Then take this.” It was the dried skeleton of a monkey’s paw.

“Of what use will it be, Gurudeva?” the puzzled disciple asked.

“Your desire will be fulfilled with this. Ask it for any three boons, and the paw will immediately grant them to you.”

He cried, “I’ve got it,” thinking that now he had something extraordinary. The disciple was beyond himself with joy. His face and eyes were brightened with expectation. By the grace of God, his devotion and services to guru had borne fruit at last! Prostrating himself before his guru, he took his leave, and started for home, a happy man.

Walking the long distance through the woods and fields, he reached home at last. Evening had already descended, spreading darkness all around. His patience was almost finished. He could not rest before he tested his new supernatural power before the whole village. He was so impatient that as soon as he crossed the threshold of his courtyard, he called all his relatives and friends to come and see.

Overwhelmed with joy, he said, “Look, all of you! My long penance at my guru’s *áshram* bore fruit at last. I am

now in possession of a wonderful, rare, supernatural power. Here is that great power – the monkey's paw – given to me by my *gurudeva*." He flourished the paw and with great enthusiasm addressed it, "O paw! Bring me a hundred thousand rupees right now!"

A gift from the guru. It must be effective. Yes, there was somebody approaching and knocking at the door.

"Who is there inside? I am here from the government. Open the door, please."

The man thought, "Yes, this person is calling for me."

He threw the door open, and a man entered the room. Introducing himself as a government messenger, the man told him that his son, who had been in the navy, had died in a shipwreck, and he, the messenger, was there to convey to him that he was to get all the dues of his son, including his savings, amounting to Rs. 100,000.

Shock! "Oh God! What have I done! I lost my only son, dearer than my own self, in exchange for rupees! No, no, it can't be! I want my son back. Give him back!" He appealed to the magic paw, "O magic paw! I want my son. Bring him back to me, please!"

Once lost in time, nothing can be brought back, by any means. That is the irrevocable law of nature – a very hard and bitter truth. No prayer is of avail here. Even the heart-rending appeal of a grief-stricken father rebounds from the stone wall of the law, echoing, "It is no more! It is no more!"

### Keeping His Word

I once went to visit Ácárya Rameswar Baitha in Patna. He was one of the senior-most disciples of Bábá; he was initiated directly by Bábá in 1955. That day he told me the following story.

I should like to mention here that many of Bábá's close disciples, especially in those early intimate days, would not make any major decision without His advice and suggestions. The devotees considered Bábá like the head of their own family. They looked to Him as their own dearest, and He was one of them.

Sometimes He would show up at a disciple's home. This sudden arrival would throw the whole household into a frenzy of excitement. "Bábá has come! Bábá is here!" would echo through the house. Bábá would sit with them and ask

about everyone's health and well-being. He would talk for a while with them. The devotees felt free to tell all their personal and domestic problems – when and where the son should be enrolled, how to proceed with the daughter's marriage arrangements, whether to accept a job – and Bábá would listen to everyone. He consoled them in their sorrows, rejoiced with them in their happiness and advised them in all activities of their lives.

In this way, Rameswarjii, with a heavy heart, went to Bábá and told Him everything about the transfer. Bábá said, "As it is, it's better you go to Danapur."

Rameswarjii was stunned. He did not expect such advice from Bábá. "Bábá," he said, "you know my salary is very low. I have to sustain my family with great difficulty and hardship. So long as I am here, I can attend to my job from my home. But at Danapur, I will have to rent a house. That will take away a considerable part of my income. How shall I support my family?"

"Who will take care of your family," Bábá said seriously, "you or me?"

Ác. Rameswar had nothing left to say after this. He thought of Bábá as the real head of his family. His family would be perfectly secure from all sorts of troubles under Bábá's all-pervasive loving care.

Bábá's concern for His devotees and disciples was not limited to the sphere of spirituality alone. He took care of their psychic well-being, and He also saw to their all-round development from the economic, social and physical viewpoint.

However, he had one concern. He asked, "Does it mean, Bábá, that I have to leave all behind and be away from you?"

"What makes you think so?" was Bábá's sympathetic answer. "Why worry? Wherever you are, I will always be with you."

So, in obedience to Bábá's advice, Ác. Rameswarjii left with reluctance for Danapur. Some years later, Bábá arrived at Danapur to conduct *dharma mahácakra*. Ác. Rameswar was among those present to welcome Him at the airport. Alighting from the plane, Bábá went straight to him.

With a loving smile playing on His lips, He said, "Rameswar, see, I have come to your Danapur!"

Ác. Rameswar remembered Bábá's promises in Jamalpur, and these loving words from Him touched his heart deeply. His eyes glistened with joy.

Again Ác. Rameswar was transferred, this time to Purnea. Later Bábá flew to Purnea on a chartered flight for DMC. Again Ác. Rameswar was there with the others at the airport. As Bábá walked down to the gate, He suddenly saw Rameswarjii. He stopped in front of him and Bábá smiled enchantingly, "Rameswar, you see, here I am with you in Purnea, too!"

After some more years, Ác. Rameswar was once again transferred – this time to Jamshedpur, and again Bábá visited to conduct a DMC. On His arrival at the airport, Bábá reminded Rameswarjii, as on previous occasions, "I am here with you at your Jamshedpur, also." Rameswarjii again wept in joy.

Many people are in the habit of giving promises that eventually get lost. Bábá would never forget a promise He had given to anybody, even after long years. Bábá not only kept His promise to be present with Ác. Rameswar, but took upon Himself the responsibility of running his family smoothly.

Ác. Rameswar finished telling me his story with tears of devotion in his eyes and emotion filling his voice. He is now peacefully retired and living in Patna in his own house. All

of his daughters have married well and his only son is an IFS officer in the government. Everyone is settled and happy simply due to Bábá’s blessing. The Supreme Lord has given him a place in the shade of His lotus feet and has showered His infinite grace on him.

### Ever-Loving Care

Bábá’s care extended in ways we could not always fathom. An unforgettable incident happened in the late 1960’s. We were in Ranchi, then headquarters of our mission, and I was the general secretary.

A new *brahmacárii* worker was to be assigned to Baharampur as Dit. S. (district secretary), which according to our structure falls within Krishnanagar Diocese. Bábá summoned me suddenly. He asked me to accompany that him to Baharampur that very day.

Although I nodded my assent, the order took me by surprise. In the first place, a GS has to deal with innumerable things, overseeing the functioning of the whole mission. So why would Bábá be sending the GS on such an errand – leaving all his work unattended? The new Dit. S. was to take over the office at Baharampur and there were others with experience available there – any one of them could easily have done the job.

Secondly, Krishnanagar was my own area. I grew up and was educated there. According to our workers’ rules, one should not return to one’s own locality except in an emergency.

However, Bábá’s directive is to be obeyed without questions. I prostrated myself before Him and took my leave to start immediately. As soon as I stood up, Bábá said, “Listen, from here go first to Krishnanagar. From there, the diocese secretary, of course, will join you. Moreover, take Manasmohan along with you.”

“Yes, Bábá, it will be done,” I replied and left the room. But I was all the more perplexed. The instruction to take with us the local family *ácárya*, along with the DS, was something of a puzzle. As far as I knew, Ác. Manasmohan was not familiar with Baharampur. Besides, he held a government job; it was doubtful whether he could leave the office on such short notice. Moreover the DS and I were fairly familiar with the area. What sense there was in taking Ác. Manasmohan with us was beyond my comprehension.

“Why does Bábá want Manasmohanji to accompany us?” I thought. Anyhow, I kept quiet and decided I would take that action if I saw it necessary. With these thoughts in my mind, I had gone only a few steps outside Bábá’s room. A worker came running after me saying that Bábá was calling me. Hurriedly I turned back.

Very seriously Bábá asked me, “Do you understand what you have been asked to do?”

“Yes, Bábá, I do,” I humbly replied.

“ ‘Yes, Bábá, I do’ is not enough,” He said scathingly, “Make sure to pick up Ác. Manasmohan from Krishnanagar before you leave for Baharampur. Don’t forget.”

I nodded in silence, and came out of the room. It was clear to me that my doubts and thinking about using my own judgement in my future actions were instantly conveyed to Bábá’s mind. Therefore, He had felt it necessary to emphasize His orders in a way that I could not ignore. A saint, Maharśi Cavana, once said, *Bheśajaḿ hitavákyaḿ ca tiktaḿ áshu phalapradam*, which means, “Medicines and good advice become more effective if they are somewhat bitter”. Anyway, an omniscient, all-powerful being, who can see the minds of all, must have some reason behind each word He

speaks. There was no longer any question in my mind that I should obey Bábá’s instructions.

Taking the new Dit. S. with me, we reached our Ananda Marga school at Krishnanagar the next day around ten in the morning. I explained to the DS all about the new posting at Baharampur and Bábá’s instructions. Accordingly, immediately after our lunch, the three of us went to Ác. Manasmohan’s office. In a few minutes we were meeting with Manasmohanji. After he heard that he had been asked to go to Baharampur that very day, he was taken aback. Reacting sharply, he exclaimed, “Impossible! I don’t have any leave due, and on no pretext can any leave be obtained at this moment because there is no one to substitute for me. The DS is going with you, and you two are well-acquainted with the place. I have never been there and am a total stranger there, so what is the point of my going?”

Ác. Manasmohan sounded reasonable and his logic was undoubtedly convincing. But I recalled Bábá’s second summons and His categorical instructions. Had Bábá not been so adamant, I could easily have been swayed by Ác. Manasmohan’s reasoning, and we two – the DS and myself – would have taken the Dit. S. to Baharampur ourselves.

I did not think twice. I told him firmly, “It is Bábá’s clear order; you must come. Whatever the difficulties and inconveniences are, Bábá’s order must be carried out completely. It’s true what you say is perfectly sensible and convincing, but I’m sorry, I can’t accept it. By Bábá’s order, by any means you are to accompany us.”

Ác. Manasmohan, of course, was very devoted to Bábá. It did not take him any time to realize that this was a supreme command. He had nothing to do but to obey it and start for Baharampur. So without further delay, he came with us, after forwarding a formal application for leave to his superior.

The four of us came to the Krishnanagar railway station. The Baharampur-bound local was not to arrive yet for an hour. We sat on a bench on the platform and engaged ourselves in conversation. Some unknown men, standing some distance away, attracted our attention. They were looking at us suspiciously and talking among themselves. We did not think anything of it because gossip and conjecture about Ananda Marga was not unusual in those days. Everybody judged us from his own point of view. This was nothing unusual.

The train for Baharampur arrived on time. We were quick to get in and occupied some seats. Within half an hour the train reached Dhubulia station where it was to stop for a few minutes. As soon as the train stopped, some men crashed into our compartment and began attacking us, shouting at the top of their voices, “You hypocrite *sádhus*! Kidnappers! Get down from the carriage, quick!” Grabbing our arms, they were about to drag us out of the compartment. Their violent appearance, the smell of liquor on their breaths, their crude language and beastly behaviour showed they were from some antisocial gang. There was no doubt that their actions were premeditated; they attacked us with definite motive. We were nothing before their combined force; we were totally helpless. Even then, our moral courage was decidedly stronger than theirs. We stood in defiant protest against their onslaught.

We challenged them, “Who are you? Why should we listen to you? We’re not going anywhere. Get out!”

Now, encouraged by our moral strength, some of the other passengers came to our aid. They strongly protested against the thugs, after a little scuffle left the compartment with threats. They shouted, “You won’t get out? We’ll see if the train moves from here!”

Since I came from Krishnanagar, every nook and cranny of the district was known to me. Thousands of refugees from then East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) lived in Dhubulia. Each family received a dole from the government every month. Most of them were unemployed and had no means of subsistence. An idle mind is the devil's workshop, and indiscriminate plundering, robbery, child-snatching, rape, theft and murder, even in broad daylight, were common in this area. Dhubulia was well-known to everybody as a notorious station.

Therefore, such a threat from these miscreants could not be taken lightly. I sensed danger. Propaganda branding the *sannyásiis* (monks) of Ananda Marga as kidnappers had been conducted by the communists in the past, too. It would not be difficult here for these people to mislead the general public with the same accusations.

I told Manasmohanji, "These men are not going to stop at this, they will not hesitate to pull the emergency alarm chain. They are sure to come back with their whole gang and then the situation will be out of control. Please, don't disclose your identity. Don't let them know you are one of us and a Margi. As for us, Bábá will do what is necessary."

We handed over all the important papers and documents of the mission to Ác. Manasmohan. My intuition was active at that moment, and I told Ác. Manasmohan that in any eventuality, he should use his own judgement, and in the name of Bábá, take whatever action he thought necessary.

In the meantime, the train had moved a little distance from the station, but suddenly came to a creaking halt. The gangsters had pulled the alarm chain. Instantly a large contingent of ruffians broke into our compartment, shouting filthy, abusive language. This time their only aim was to haul us off the train. They used their combined force and started to drag us by the arms. They were mad in their bestial behaviour. The other passengers were frozen in terror.

Some ladies were trembling and broke into tears. They stood and with pleading hands implored, "Swamijiis, please get down, all of you. These people will go to any lengths. They will not hesitate to set the whole train into flames if they don't get their way. No one will be able to save us then. Such crimes are regular affairs in this area. Swamijii, don't delay, please."

Suddenly, something flashed in my mind. There was only one who could save us from this predicament. Closing my eyes for a few seconds, I concentrated my mind on Bábá. I regained my strength and equanimity.

Opening my eyes I asked our attackers, "Well, where do you want us to go? Let us go there."

Saying this, we three *sannyásiis* promptly got down from the compartment. On the platform we faced a huge crowd of hundreds of people. They pounced upon us immediately and started heckling us. They pulled off our turbans and tried to tear our clothes off us, pushing and pulling at us from all sides, shouting the same as before, "Kidnappers! Beat them! Beat them!" They were like a pack of frenzied wolves, it was quite useless to inquire which boy, whose son, where, who. I realized that we were the victims of a planned conspiracy. I again remembered my saviour Bábá and surrendered to Him.

No sooner had we gotten down from the train, than it whistled away. Who had pulled the chain and why, why passengers had been dragged out, and what their offense was—such inquiries were the duty of the railway authorities. But in this case they were not to be.

We were dragged forcefully along the railway lines, the gang hitting us with all their might. They brought us to a big

field adjacent to the station. In a twinkling of an eye, thousands assembled there. Some had come with the intention of teaching some child-stealers a good lesson, some had come to get a kick out of beating us and others came to enjoy these jokes. Pulling and pushing and dragging us, we were brought, at last, to the middle of the field.

We were surrounded by angry, leering men on all sides. To them, we were only kidnappers to be brought to mob justice. Vulgar words were continuously thrown at us. Different people were threatening us with any weapon at hand – branches, bamboos broken from some fencing, iron rods. We were victims of a deep conspiracy and would be the victims of a lynching. After this not even our nameless corpses would be found. With fear in our eyes we trembled to think what lie ahead and again remembered Bábá. We consecrated ourselves to Bábá’s holy feet and stood motionless, our eyes closed, lost in thought of Him.

All of a sudden, a forceful voice rang out over the noise of the mob. “Let me pass! Give me passage, quickly! Move aside!” Pushing aside that impenetrable crowd, a handsome, robust man came striding up.

Reaching us he said “Come along, Swamijiis, come along with me. All of you, let us through.”

Mesmerized by a mysterious force, the insane mob quickly let him through. Without thinking further we followed him and within a few minutes reached an old brick house. He thrust us into a room and locked the door from the outside.

We thought we were out of danger for the time being, by the infinite grace of Bábá, and heaved a sigh of relief. Alas! It only took a short time for that mob to regain their momentum, and rushing to that spot, they surrounded the room. They started to pound and beat on the door. We had bolted the shuttered, barred windows from inside, but they were hitting them violently. Soon the latches were broken and the panels splintered. They started throwing rocks and brickbats through the broken windows; we sheltered ourselves in a corner of the room. Some of them were trying to break the iron grilles off the windows. All the while, our brave protector was trying to drive the thugs from the door and shouting to get them away from the windows. This tremendous struggle continued for a long time.

In the meantime, the plight of Ác. Manasmohan could be easily imagined. The train had departed with him still on it. His mind was a turbulent sea. How cruelly his brothers had been dragged away by those hooligans – he could hardly tolerate it. He was at a loss what to do. It would only be possible to do something after returning to Krishnanagar. And there was no means to get there. And again, there was no certainty that his dedicated brothers would survive until that time. Anyhow he would manage to reach Krishnanagar and take some steps.

Completely overwhelmed, he thought, “Oh Lord, you put me in such a severe ordeal! Only you can find a way out. My brain is not working. Guide me.”

The train stopped at the next station. Ác. Manasmohan quickly got down. As soon as he set his feet on the platform, he found that a Krishnanagar-bound train was standing on the other side. It was an express train running late. He got on and it left immediately. The train soon reached the Krishnanagar station. Jumping down onto the platform, Ác. Manasmohan rushed to the office of the police superintendent. There he quickly registered a report that a gang of hooligans had snatched away the general secretary of Ananda Marga along with two other *sannyásiis* at Dhubulia station. He narrated in detail all that he had seen.

Shocking as it is to us to undergo such an experience such things are not uncommon to the police in India, especially in Nadia District, and particularly at Dhubulia. So it was hope against hope that the police would take prompt steps on hearing such things. Often in such cases, the police do not accept the report at all because it means unnecessary trouble for them running on a wild goose chase. The police would have to play an active role, hurrying to the spot with an armed force and wasting government money. The police often become active only after the incident passes away smoothly. It seems they do not feel obliged to detect and arrest miscreants. In this situation, especially in the present communist-ruled state government, it might happen like that. Information given by a particular organization against the supporters of the ruling party – it would be very serious thing, indeed, got the police if involved. Then the police themselves would be in a tight corner. They would stand guilty and face questions by their political as well as departmental bosses about their actions. They would be put to much trouble.

But thank God, the superintendent of police became active as soon as he got the report. (I can think of three reasons why the police acted against expectations: one, the administrative skill and sincerity of the police superintendent came into play; two, the name of the general secretary made them active; and three, the authorities' good conscience was awakened by an unseen influence.) On the superintendent's orders, two vans of police armed with tear gas and other riot weapons set out for Dhubulia within a few minutes. They drove at tremendous speed and reached the spot within a short time. Seeing such a large number of police getting down from the van, the mob dispersed in a few seconds. The police unlocked the room and rescued us.

A policeman showed us to the van, and we sat down. Ác. Manasmohan had come along with the police and now joined us. We gave a brief sketch of what had happened to the police. The police collected information from the locals and conducted an investigation of the crime.

They took us safely back to Krishnanagar. We did not forget to give our hearty thanks to the police superintendent and the other officers for rescuing us from the claws of the mob.

The Supreme Father snatched us from the jaws of death when escape seemed impossible. I felt we all got new lives handed to us. Tears rolled down my cheeks as I offered my homage to Him for His kindness and mercy. Had He not called me a second time that day and emphasized in a stern voice taking Ác. Manasmohan with us, had He not made proper connections among the intricate maze of successive happenings, had the police reached the spot a little later – then there is not the least doubt that our lives would have been forfeit. But nobody can snatch the Lord's loving children from the ever-secure shelter of His feet without His consent. It was true that day, it remains true today and it will remain true in the future, also.

But a mystery remains. Who was that man of steel personality? How was he able to penetrate that violent mob and

Returning to Ranchi, I went straight to Bábá's room and stood up after offering *prańám*. Before I could say anything, Bábá smiled mysteriously and lovingly asked, "My dear son, I hope you didn't forget to pick up Ác. Manasmohan from Krishnanagar?"

CHAPTER 17

# THOSE TWO EYES

It has been said that guru is *nigrahánugraha shakta*, that is, the guru is one who not only loves his or her disciples, but also disciplines them for their all-round development. This is because the waywardness of human nature sometimes needs strong guidance to lead it on to a higher existence. There are so many stories of different masters who lived in different ages that have guided their disciples with severe hand as well as a loving touch. Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti was a rare, extraordinary teacher, full of compassion for and deep insight into human nature. His omniscient nature was not restricted to merely being a watchman over our faults. He watched His disciples grow as a mother watched over her children. Affection was there, and chastisement too. For the devotee the relationship with Supreme Father was made in a very personal and practical way. We realized the personal nature of the Supreme through our loving Bábá.

As GS, I was to take the daily reports of the workers and submit them to Bábá. Our organization is extensive with several thousand workers and volunteers connected with it. Thus, it was not possible to check on the performance of every worker all the time. So, a schedule was prepared. Each department, sector, region or diocese of the mission was assigned to a fixed day in the month for presenting their reports. This system was very effective for getting a regular overview of everything going on. To facilitate the work, everybody followed this routine. This is the system of the organization, and it works well.

I used to collect the progress reports, and then I would

submit a brief summary of them to Bábá. After going through my report meticulously, Bábá might call some of the workers if necessary. If the performance was unsatisfactory, He would ask for explanation, and would rebuke and admonish the worker severely. It is true that though His main purpose was to teach something to the worker, indirectly we would learn something, too, observing the exchange. It was killing two birds with one stone.

Although Bábá never did anything superficially, neither did He ever give any forewarning of what He was going to check up on next. Needless to say, He invariably picked a worker for questioning who had committed some mistake or who was guilty of some dereliction of duty. To do this no elaborate cross-examination was necessary. Nor did it take much time. He would ask one or two questions, and within minutes some fault would be exposed. To be caught in this way was possible not only in relation to organizational duties. Personal cleanliness, purity of mind, efficiency and manners also came under Bábá's scrutiny. He often did not make much of the organizational reports in themselves, but used them as a point of departure for taking an X-ray of the conduct of a worker's personal life. For that, He needed no instruments or mechanical devices. Looking intently at anybody, He could see vividly someone's past, present and future. Generally He would not discuss these things unless it was absolutely needed for the good of that person. That was His usual practice.

In this respect, He once said, "When I look at a person, I instantly come to know all about him or her: his or her past, present and future, his or her outer and inner selves are revealed to me all at once. But none of this should be publicly exposed. That may lower him or her in the estimation of others, and that is not desirable. Everyone has some dark spots in his or her past. Similarly there is a bright future for all. So the right approach should be to make him or her realize his or her faults and mistakes, so that they do not occur again, and to help him or her move towards a glorious future."

This happened in the early eighties: Bábá was staying at Lake Gardens in Calcutta. One day we were in the middle of a reporting session in the office. All the workers of Delhi Sector were present. I was there to assist Bábá. That day, after completing His scrutiny of the daily reports, Bábá started reviewing some of the workers' sincerity in adhering to the Sixteen Points. On Bábá's instructions, I called a new worker forward.

I asked, "You follow the Sixteen Points and maintain all sorts of cleanliness, I think?"

Before the young worker could say anything, Bábá, who had been silent so far, suddenly interrupted, "Tell me, do you really follow the principles of cleanliness? Try to remember a day two months ago, you were travelling from Tatanagar to a village two miles away around noontime. On the way, you had to urinate, but you did not use water to wash yourself clean. What made you think that there was no one who could see if you did not use water after urinating? Is this your adherence to the principles of cleanliness? Don't you know that what you were thinking at the time and what you did are clearly imprinted in my mind."

"Yes, Bábá," he confessed, "I violated that rule and entertained those thoughts."

"Was that the only time you violated the Sixteen Points? Just try to remember. Exactly three months and ten days back, you were in Assam. It was winter and very cold. Afraid of the cold, you did not take a bath; you did *vyápak shaoca* haphazardly before eating your meal. But is this the way to

take a bath according to our disciplines? One must take full bath at least once a day. If however the temperature is 100° F (37° C) or higher, one should bathe at least twice a day, three or four times is better. Well, that day you did not observe this rule at all.” The young worker admitted his fault and begged to be pardoned.

“You see,” Bábá continued, “on many other occasions you violated the Sixteen Points. Take the case of just the other day. It was *ekádashii*, and towards evening you felt a little thirsty. You thought, ‘What harm can it be to take a little water?’ Others make a determined effort to observe this day with as much sanctity as possible. You did nothing like that; you did not try to control your thirst. Rather you thought, ‘Nobody will ever know if I take water surreptitiously.’ Isn’t it so?”

The worker completely broke down. Bábá was fully aware of the ins and outs of his life, and to do anything to evade Him was impossible. All the young workers present got a visible lesson that Bábá’s ever-watchful eyes were taking note of all their activities.

Now Bábá sternly asked him, “Tell me. Never in the future shall you entertain such immoral thoughts! Will you ever violate the rules of righteous conduct in the future? Are you going to show any more lack of discipline in observing the Sixteen Points?”

Repentant, the worker kept on repeating, “No, Bábá, from now on I will be very careful about discipline and strictly follow the Sixteen Points.”

“From when?”

“From today.”

“No, not from today, say ‘from right now’.”

The worker repeated quickly, “Yes, Bábá, from right now.”

Bábá was silent.

As instructed by Bábá, some more young workers were called, one by one. I asked them whether they were observing the comprehensive rules of cleanliness, regular bath, *sáttvik* food, necessary fasting, and regular spiritual practices. They replied affirmatively, saying that they had been trying to follow the rules as best they could. I noticed that Bábá made no comment about any of them, but said, “Well, be on your guard. Let there be no slackness in your meditation or in the performance of your organizational duties. Follow the Sixteen Points meticulously with wholehearted attention. It will be good for you.”

In those years, Bábá used to lay great emphasis on the Sixteen Points: “Concentrate your moral forces unitedly, and all the influences of evil forces will stop.” All Margis became more careful in following those points as taught by Bábá. If any worker was found to be negligent in his or her duties, Bábá would not spare him or her, but give a stern rebuke.

I frequently noticed that often during reporting sessions of three to four hundred workers or Margis, Bábá would suddenly pick out one from the group and start questioning him or her regarding the Sixteen Points.

One such case was a block secretary (UBP). Bábá asked him, “Did you properly wash with soap your undergarments while taking your bath the day before yesterday? After your bath, I noticed you put on the used clothes again. Don’t you notice that a garment soaked in sweat for more than twenty-four hours generates an offensive smell? You are a cultured, responsible person. What will others think of you, if you move among them with such a stench?”

“Yes, Bábá,” was the embarrassed reply, “the day before

yesterday I could not wash it because I was in a hurry.” He promised to follow the Sixteen Points with more sincerity after that.

Bábá called another UBP forward and asked, “Well, tell me – what *ásanas* did you practise in the evening the day before yesterday?” And without waiting for a reply, Bábá continued, “You did not practise any *ásanas* that evening. After doing a little routine meditation, you got up and went out on some pretext.”

“Yes Bábá, I did no *ásanas* that day. I had no time,” was his excuse.

“You say there was no time! Does that mean you stopped breathing because there was no time, or didn’t have time to take your meal?”

He mumbled, “No, Bábá, nothing like that happened.”

“If you could manage to do these things properly, why not *ásanas*? Don’t you know that I don’t tolerate any slackness in the Sixteen Points, however little it might be! I don’t want work from anybody who does not strictly observe discipline.”

That UBP had nothing to say. Bábá was strict about observing the Sixteen Points. The Margi stood silently, with his eyes to the floor.

Bábá turned towards us. Ruefully He said, “How much more should I tell? The problem with me is that I know everything of everybody’s life and activities. Even then, is it wise to expose all the faults and shortcomings of everyone?”

Bábá took the effort to point out these lapses in our spiritual practices only out of loving concern for our own development. So we humbly accepted everything He said about the importance of spiritual disciplines.

Bábá turned back to the *ásana* neglector. “Did you practise *dhyána* last night?” The secretary thought for a moment and remembered that really he had not done *dhyána* at all the previous night.

“No, Bábá, I had an urgent engagement at that time and had therefore to complete my meditation in a hurry. So it was a mere formality.”

“No,” Bábá said, “I am not going to accept your explanation. In fact, you did not sit for meditation at all, no vibration of meditation was felt by me. Properly or superficially, in whatever manner meditation is done, its vibration invariably reaches me.”

Through His *antaryámitva*, Bábá knew every activity of His devotees and disciples. He was aware of what they were thinking or doing at any time, and so was quite specific in His questions: “What made you do such-and-such thing at such-and-such time?” If anyone would ever try to wriggle his or her way out of it, Bábá would press him or her hard, painting a vivid picture of the specifics – when, where, the thoughts, with whom – just like pictures on a movie screen. The offender then had no alternative but to surrender completely to Him.

To err is human, but nevertheless Bábá would point errors out to us. He made us feel in the core of our hearts that a *sádhaka* has to rise above common failings. All the unhealthy, blinding dust on the path must be swept clean to facilitate steady progress towards our ideal. Whatever Bábá did or said was only to hammer this truth into our minds.

This was the technique that Bábá applied to check on the *sádhakas* – how far they were following the Sixteen Points. And this process went on regularly: someone omitted singing *kiirtan* before their meditation, someone did not sit erect while meditating, someone did not do proper massage or *shavásana* after *ásanas*. Not only this, but also, the wander-

ing thoughts playing in the minds of the devotees and aspirants during meditation did not escape Bábá’s notice.

Bábá was not satisfied to simply point out the defects of the devotees in this way. He was concerned with all sorts of physical and mental aberrations that might afflict them. In such cases, He would suggest proper remedial measures, such as *ásanas* specific for the ailments. He would prescribe a complete diet, clearly indicating what should be eaten and the quantity, as well as the items to avoid. In this way, Bábá ensured the all-round development of the personality of the devotees including the physical, mental and spiritual aspect of his life.

One day in a reporting session, Bábá’s eyes fell upon a particular worker. The worker came forward, and Bábá made a casual check of his organizational work. Then He looked with His inner eyes and saw a number of defects. A disease was diagnosed, but no treatment can be effective if one does not get to the root cause. Many times there are deep psychological causes. Bábá’s method would be to shed light on those causes in order to chase those dark shadows away permanently.

Bábá said, “About four years ago, when you were about nineteen or twenty, you could not secure a job despite your qualifications. You had to suffer the worst type of humiliation and insulting reproaches on that account. Do you remember at noon, you came home one day hungry, completely exhausted from wandering aimlessly in the scorching sun? Not a single morsel had you eaten since morning, and your stomach was burning. You had an idea that you would get something to eat after a preliminary wash. But as soon as you entered the house, your stepmother flared up in anger and started shouting abuse and bitter curses, ‘You worthless fellow, good-for-nothing vagabond! The only thing you know to do is to idle away your time and eat like a demon! Do you expect me to believe no job is available? No, that’s a lame excuse. So many people are getting jobs, why can’t you get one? You are a despicable animal!’”

The dark days of his past came painfully back to the worker’s mind. He hung his head and wept. He had never told anyone of the sufferings he had undergone, for years he had borne silently that pain. How did Bábá know it!

After a brief pause, Bábá said, “You must be thinking of the inhuman treatment meted out by your stepmother – the unbearable pain in the heart for so many days, the sleepless nights passed in weeping, the pillow soaked with tears. Restless with intense agony, you passed the days helplessly looking for something to hold onto. ‘What a wretched creature am I! What an object of contempt!’ you used to think. ‘Is there no one who cares enough to help me? Oh God! So heartless You are! So blind! Am I to end like this? No, there is no God or any such thing. It is all false, nothing but illusion.’ ”

Now the worker completely broke down. Every word Bábá uttered depicted his true inner feelings that he had not expressed to anyone. But Bábá is within our heart of hearts: the more he thought of Bábá’s compassion, the more pronounced his sobbing.

Bábá continued, “You don’t know how much I felt for the miserable condition of your life that day. I thought to myself ‘What a pity, a simple innocent boy has to suffer such ignoble humiliation and agony like a criminal standing in the witness box with no one to plead for him.’ Then and there, I decided to bring you into my mission as a wholetime worker. That put an end to your constant worries and humiliation. You are able to move freely with your head held

high in dignity.”

“I am sure,” Bábá added, “you remember that you came across an *ácárya* a few days later. After that, you were taught meditation. You started practising regularly, and in due course joined the mission as a worker. Today you have gained the life of an exalted spiritual being, free from sorrow and mental tension. Your only principle now is to dedicate yourself to the service of humanity. The fact that you have joined the Marga is not an accident because every event in this world is causally determined.”

Bábá signalled him to come closer, then in a deep sweet voice, asked, “Tell me now, are you an object of contempt? Doesn’t anybody love you as His own?”

Such a question is not meant to be answered. The only reply was unspoken tears of love, through which he could express his feelings. Slowly, he regained his poise and stopped weeping.

Bábá then said, “Everything else is okay with you. But what made you violate the Sixteen Points?”

He could not recollect what Bábá was referring to and was trying hard to remember. Bábá did not give him time, He supplied the cue. “Can’t you remember? It was three years, two months and four days back. It was late afternoon. You were sitting alone in a room in the corner of your village home. Unnoticed by anybody, you shaved clean your pubic hair. ‘Who will see it,’ you thought. Isn’t it so? Then, wrapping it in paper, you threw it out behind the house. There was a big tree there, remember?”

“Yes, Bábá. It is true, but that was before I was initiated. I was not a Margi then and didn’t know the Sixteen Points.”

“True, you were not within the Marga. But does that mean I should not take interest in your activities or be ignorant of your faults and lapses?” Bábá added ironically, “Anyway, you may be excused on those grounds. But in any case, a fault is a fault after all!”

After a little pause, Bábá continued, “Well, what was that tree behind your house? Wasn’t it a hog plum?”

“Yes, Bábá, it was, indeed, a hog plum.”

“Very well, is that tree still alive?…No, how could you know that? The tree is no longer alive. It died a year ago.”

Again, after a pause, Bábá said, “Well, when you threw the packet away, you were looking around to see if anybody was watching you, and were relieved when you were certain that there wasn’t anyone. Can you remember that a small bird flew overhead at that moment? What bird was that? Was that a sparrow?”

“Yes, Bábá, a sparrow,” he readily agreed.

“Do you know who, in reality, that bird was? He took note of everything you had been doing and, flying directly to me, made a report of what he had seen – ‘Bábá, that boy of yours has done so-and-so at such-and-such a time.’ Thank goodness the bird gave me the information! How else could I know so much, staying as I do within these four walls of Lake Gardens?”

Bábá’s narration of the bird reporting to Him – as if He would not know it otherwise – made us smile. The worker grasped the point. “No, Bábá, You don’t need any medium; everything is open to your view. You are omniscient.”

Bábá changed moods. The role of strict disciplinarian and stern administrator gave way to the enchanting, loving Father.

“Do you know, my children, the source of my knowledge?”

“No, Bábá, we would like to know that very much,” we eagerly replied.

“There is a pair of eyes watching all of you from behind.

All your acts and deeds – big and small – are constantly being observed by those two eyes, and are instantly being reported to me. It goes on ceaselessly, even when I am asleep. It is not merely what you are doing; every detail of your thoughts, your imagination, etc., is silently whispered into my ears. These two eyes are very responsible. They are never idle nor are they unmindful of their duties for even a moment. Naturally, the regular supply of information keeps me well-posted on what’s going on. But you people are not aware of these eyes. Even if you try to see them by quickly twisting your head around, they shift their position from back to front.”

“Now tell me,” Bábá asked, “why are these eyes behind, and not in front?”

“What is the harm if they are in the front” we asked in return.

“No,” Bábá explained, “That would unnerve you. The worry of your faults and lapses being detected and being reported to me would haunt you all the time. As a result, the normal behaviour of your life will be seriously disturbed.”

Humorously, Bábá continued, “Now that you know that these twin eyes are watching from behind, it will be a constant source of uneasiness. I know you are thinking, ‘What a nuisance! Whatever we are doing is being reported to Bábá!’ Isn’t it?”

“No, Bábá, they are for our good, we know. They are not merely keeping watch over our activities. They are with us, as guide and protector. Whenever we are in any danger or in a position that might be harmful to us, they are there to rescue us and to lead us safely on the right path.”

“Think over it, all of you,” Bábá spoke with mystic humour. “If you want those eyes to be removed from your back, I am prepared to consider it.”

In spontaneous unison we replied, “No, Bábá, no! Please don’t take them away. We want them with us all the time”

Bábá laughed, “Whether you want it or not, those eyes will stay with you and do their job unnoticed for all time to come. Even if you were unanimously in favour of withdrawing them, I would not oblige you.”

True, those two mysterious, watchful eyes are always with us now and they will always be with us. At one time we could actually see them eyes before our own and rejoice in the dancing light shining from them. Those special eyes are no longer in our physical vision. They are gone forever, and only the bright memory is shining in our minds.

No – it’s not just a memory. His active presence is felt in every fibre of my being. Whenever a dark thought gathers in my mind, when I lose my way, at a loss to know what to do, suddenly a gust of wind blows through my mind, clearing the mists for new rays of hope to penetrate. The soft, white, transparent moonlight of the deep night then smiles on me again with its brilliance. Although my conscience may sometimes neglect to notice my errors, those ever-vigilant eyes never miss a thing. They send me a stern warning and awaken my conscience. My eyes glisten with joy whenever I remember that blissful being with those enchanting eyes, the unfailing guide of my life forevermore.

# UNDER THE SHELTER OF THE SUPREME

The life of a human being on this planet is short. Your days are numbered, and in that short time you have to face innumerable obstacles and hardships; you must struggle hard to survive. Sometimes you will be in deep crisis, and sometimes your very existence will be at stake in the terrific storm of life. If you are lucky, you weather the calamities thanks to the unseen hands of *Parama Puruśa*, and again you are able to move in harmony on your life’s journey.

Who is this unknown eternal Being whose compassion has made all this possible? What exactly is His nature; how can He be known? Is He unreachable like a star in the sky beyond?

No, He is not a transcendental being beyond reach; neither is He an illusion. He is in the innermost depths of your soul, an ever-shining spirit that can be felt by personal experience. He shares your pain and suffering. A great compassionate One, He is the friend of the downtrodden, the saviour of the distressed and the guiding light to the lost!

### I Am for All

After conducting DMC at Tatanagar, on 29 July 1967, Bábá was going to Raipur; another DMC was to be held there. I had the privilege of accompanying Him as His PA. We were scheduled to travel by the Howrah-Bombay Mail. We reached the Tatanagar railway station at about nine in the morning. A large number of devotees had assembled at the station to see us off. Shortly the train arrived, and Bábá and I took our places in first-class. The train whistled off on time. The devotees emotionally bade farewell to Bábá.

Our compartment had four berths, one upper and one lower on each side. Bábá and I were on one side occupying the upper and lower berths. On the other two berths was a couple with a son about eleven years old. Some time after the train had left the station, it started to rain heavily. The train sped on through the rain. At every station, hundreds of Margis crowded around the window of our compartment, greeting Bábá with garlands. Drenched from heavy rains, these enthusiastic devotees offered homage to Bábá, shouting, “*Param pitá Bábá ki jai!* Victory to the Supreme Father, Bábá!” Bábá accepted the garlands with His own hands. I took the garlands from Him and set them aside carefully in a basket. When the train started, they bade us farewell with tearful eyes, and shouted, “Victory to Bábá!”

The same thing was repeated at every station. Our fellow passengers witnessed these scenes with greater and greater curiosity. Who is this man? They were growing more and more interested. The elevated personality of Bábá impressed them deeply.

It was an extremely hot night. Although it was raining outside, the atmosphere inside the compartment was uncomfortable and suffocating. Bábá did not feel well. It was a first class compartment, but the amenities were not at all satisfactory. The electric fans were out of order, and we did not have even a hand fan with us to ease Bábá’s discomfort. It was so stuffy and hot, Bábá did not eat in the night. The strain of the tight schedule of the DMC in Tatanagar had been heavy. Now on top of that was this uncomfortable train. Bábá was completely exhausted and could not rest at all. All through the night I sat by His side. There was nothing to be done in the circumstances.

At last the ordeal was over, it was morning.

The train was speeding along. By late in the morning we were nearing the Bilaspur station. The couple and their son were to get down there. They were ready, their luggage fully packed. Bábá was still lying down. Suddenly He called me and asked me to bring Him the best of the red roses from the basket. I reached for one beautiful red rose and gave it to Him. He held the rose inside His folded palms and closed His eyes. After a few seconds, He put the rose in my hands and said, “Give it to the lady and ask her to preserve it carefully until her son turns twelve. After he passes his twelfth year, she may keep it or not, as she likes.”

The husband and wife were listening intently all the time He was telling me this. As I passed the flower to her, she stretched both her hands out and with intense emotion received the flower reverentially. Then I explained to her the message. Both of them expressed their gratitude to Bábá over and over again. They were shining with joy.

After some time, I left the compartment to wash my hands. The gentleman ran after me. He wanted to tell me something which he could not find courage to say to Bábá. Now catching me alone, he was overwhelmed with uncontrollable emotion. With humility and deep gratitude, he said, “Swamijii, I do not know how to repay our debt to Bábá. This is our only child, dearer to us than our own lives. Yet his horoscope indicates a serious crisis before he is twelve, and it may not be possible to overcome it. If he does not survive, our family will be doomed. However, we are convinced that he will pass through the crisis by Bábá’s blessing.”

“Certainly so,” I emphatically asserted. “Not only will he pass through the crisis, but your son will rise to great heights in his life. Your family will be happy and peaceful.”

Before I could finish, the gentleman hugged me with joy. The train was just pulling into Bilaspur station. Offering again their heartfelt gratitude to Bábá, they got down from the train and left the station.

Once again the train sped along. We two were alone in the compartment. Bábá was in a cheerful mood.

“Well, have I done wrong in offering them a flower? What do you think?” Bábá’s tone was full of affection. He sounded apologetic though.

“Why should it be wrong, Bábá? It will be a great boon to them.”

“You know, the gentleman is a very virtuous and honest man, and his wife is a pious lady. The boy is their only child, and destined before he is twelve to have a disaster that would prove fatal. I can clearly visualize that shocking event, and I felt for them. That is why I helped them. It will save the boy. But do you think I have done right in helping people outside our Marga?”

“Is there any distinction in your eyes between people belonging to the Marga and those outside it?” I asked. “Aren’t all equal and the same to you?”

Bábá was pleased. “Yes, you are right. I am for everyone. I am for all.”

I was simply overjoyed that day to see the magnanimity of Bábá’s heart. He had spent the previous night without food or sleep, and had had to undergo a heavy physical and mental strain during the recent programme. All these things might have been too much for Him, yet He wasn’t concerned about Himself. He was thinking only about the suffering of others, and how He could do something for their well-being and happiness.

### No Protector like the Lord

In 1984 when I was the rector of Ánanda Shiila, I had to come to Calcutta once for some mission work. I finished my work and was getting ready to return. The rector *didi* of Uma Nivas, the women's MU at Anandanagar, was persistently asking me to pay a visit to her project. As it is along the same route to Ranchi it would not be a problem or was an inconvenience to go there. Even my approved tour programme would not be violated; as a senior worker, I needed no special permission. Didi was constructing a new building at their centre. A huge amount of money was needed for the purpose. Proper planning, maintenance of accounts, etc. is not an easy task. She sought my help to guide her.

The request was purely innocent and sensible, but prior written permission from the head of the women's department was necessary according to our mission's regulations. Besides that, I had already informed Bábá I was to go from Calcutta directly to Ranchi. All this had to be taken into consideration before I did anything that would violate the regulations of the organization, however urgent the work might be. True, I was a senior worker, but the rules apply to everybody, so my conscience stood in the way of going without the appropriate permission. Anyway, I could not totally ignore the earnest request of the rector *didi*. The necessary permission was obtained at last. After offering my salutations to Bábá, I left for Ranchi via Uma Nivas. I reached the women's master unit in the morning and was able to complete my work there quickly. I reached nearby Kotshila at three in the afternoon, and caught the bus for Ranchi just in time.

This was an express bus. Just after we left Kotshila, it started drizzling. The road was nearly empty, so the driver geared up the speed. Suddenly the driver saw a cyclist crossing a small culvert in front of the bus on the left side of the road. To save the cyclist, the driver swerved to the right. The bus was in high gear, and as a result, the rear end swerved and crashed into the low wall of the culvert with great force; all the passengers fell headlong to the front. Some of us were thrown onto the driver, who had lost control. With a violent jerk, the bus started dragging forward with the four front wheels alone; the rear wheels had flown off with their axles. Trying to regain control, the driver swerved to the other side, turning the steering wheel a full circle. The brakes failed. The bus now skidded towards an adjacent field, and down a steep slope for approximately fifteen metres. Surely the bus was going to topple down the incline and crash to pieces. We are finished! All along, I had been silently chanting the name of the Lord, but now I remembered Bábá urgently. Shutting my eyes, I shouted, "Bábá! Bábá!"

Suddenly the bus leaped up with a jerk, and stood still on the road.

We were all dazed. Slowly regaining our senses, we heaved a sigh of relief. Some passengers in front pushed the door open. One by one, we staggered out into the open. We saw that the four rear wheels of the bus and their axles had broken their fittings and had been thrown into the field some thirty to fifty feet below us. The slope was rather slippery from the rains. The impression of the front wheels on it was clearly visible. We were mystified how a bus fully loaded with passengers could jump up so high. The accelerator had broken – it was not working at all – as soon as the axles had torn off. How could the bus have been pulled up onto the road, saving so many innocent lives?

Hundreds of people from nearby villages ran to the spot.

They all had the same question in mind: How could the bus leap up from down there? Whose grace had contrived the miracle? Everyone was awed. No one had an answer.

No, I got an answer. Some days later I found myself once again in Calcutta to attend to some work. As soon as I arrived the GS anxiously looked at me. "Well, what happened to you on such-and-such date? Were you ill? I hope you didn't have any trouble. Bábá was very disturbed and was continually asking about you, particularly around three or four in the afternoon. Seeing Bábá's so abnormally restless, we imagined something must have happened to you. What was it?"

What could I say? I replied, "There was no question of illness. I was going to be finished forever." I then told him the whole miraculous tale. "No one could have brought us back from certain death. But there was one, and only one, who could save me – and that, He did. That is why I am here, safe and alive," I said as I finished my story.

Then I started thinking. What made Him shower His mercy on me? Was it because I was a deserving disciple or was it because of Bábá's affection for me? No, it was not either of these reasons. I was saved simply because I had obeyed Bábá's instructions completely. Had I acted on my own without caring for the regulations of our mission, if I had visited the women's unit without seeking proper permission, then I myself would be the loser. No matter how much Bábá's affection for me was, His mercy might not have been there to save me.

### When Death Is Defeated

It was sometime in the 1980s. I had gone to Patna in connection with some work of the organization and was staying with Brajabiharijii, an ardent devotee of Bábá. All the members of his family, particularly his brother Kunjbiharijii, loved Bábá very much. Kunjbiharijii was at that time a top-ranking detective in the police. Bábá had a great love for both brothers, in fact, He loved the whole family.

Brajabiharijii and I were sitting in the drawing room of his house having a conversation about Bábá. In fact, whenever we were together our sole topic of conversation was Bábá. Brajabiharijii had no interest in any other subject. Filled with devotion, he would go on talking about Bábá, narrating story after story of so many events in his life. He would speak of the profound happiness he enjoys being a devotee of Bábá. His stories would make me forget myself and carry me a mystic realm. That day he told me how Bábá had saved the life of Kunjbiharijii's daughter.

Kunjbiharijii lived in Bhagalpur, a district town in Bihar. One day his daughter was cooking at the open fire when her sari caught fire. It was horrifying and the whole household came running. Unfortunately, her whole body suffered burns before the fire could be extinguished. The girl was in unbearable pain and nobody could stand to see it.

Her whole family was in complete shock. They managed to get her to the nearby government hospital. The best specialists available were brought in to treat her. All efforts were made to save her, the doctors did their best, and money was spent lavishly, but unfortunately there are cases where medical science fails. The doctors treating her gave up hope; there was no chance of her surviving. The whole family broke down at the bad news and her mother and the other women of the family started weeping inconsolably.

Both Kunjbiharijii and Brajabiharijii knew that there was only one person who could help at this stage, so they sent a relative, also a strong devotee, to Calcutta to see Bábá. But

the train arrived late that day. He was unable to reach Bábá early, and time was running out. When he arrived at Lake Gardens he saw Bábá's car waiting ready to take Him on His daily field walk.

It was indeed a wonderful opportunity, still, how to convey this sad news to Bábá? Bábá had not been feeling well recently. His PA declined to disturb Bábá with such shocking news. The PA expressed his regrets to the Margi and apologized for his inability to oblige him. The Margi was greatly disheartened. Did it mean that he had travelled all the way to Calcutta for nothing? A line from Prabhát Saḿgiita reflects his thoughts exactly, "You are there for the one who has no one else in this world."

He thought that there was not really any use in approaching a third party to convey his feelings to Bábá. Bábá is indeed omniscient. "My only duty was to come to Him," he thought. "The rest is His responsibility." The Margi surrendered himself completely. Minutes later Bábá came out of the house. He was in a cheerful mood. As usual, devotees were surrounding the car with a desire to exchange a glance or a word with Bábá. On His way to the car, He suddenly stopped and stood silently for a while. Then Bábá looked at everybody and suddenly caught sight of the Bhagalpur Margi.

Bábá turned to him and said, "How are all of you at Bhagalpur?"

The Margi thought to himself, "Surely Bábá knows. He created this situation just for this purpose." Aloud, he said to Bábá, "On the whole everything is well Bábá, but Kunjbiharijii's daughter had a serious accident and burned herself. Her condition is critical. The doctors have given up all hope of her surviving."

Bábá paused for a moment, then reassured him, "Well don't worry. Go back to Bhagalpur at once and tell them that everything will be all right. Ask them to nurse the girl with extreme care. That is all. The girl will be fine." With these words Bábá got into the car. Before it pulled away, He gave more instructions regarding the girl's treatment and other things to do. He advised them to start the treatment right away.

The Margi was relieved. If Bábá had given His assurance, even the god of death could do nothing. He returned at once, and they started the treatment advised by Bábá. Gradually, the girl improved. Soon she was no longer in critical condition, and within a few days the doctors declared her out of danger. She recovered quickly.

Brajabiharijii was a very courteous person. Though he knew his niece's recovery was only due to Bábá's intervention, he approached the chief of the medical staff to thank him.

"I am quite grateful to you," he said. "Her life has been saved only due to your sincere service and treatment."

The doctor vehemently protested. "No, no. That is not at all true. Please don't say that. I can't claim any credit in this case. Look here, see for yourself what medicine has to say in these cases." He pulled down an American medical book from the shelf and read aloud a passage.

The passage said that if a human body sustains burns of more than 65 per cent the chances of the patient surviving are not more than 5-10 per cent. And that would have been in America with advanced technology easily available. "In this case," the doctor continued, "the burn injury was 90 per cent. Besides that the facilities here are quite poor. So there was no chance at all of your girl surviving. You are very fortunate. God has been kind to you. It is nothing but a miracle."

The doctor was right. There are so many things in this world that science fails to explain. How could anyone live through what she did? There is no satisfactory answer. Only the devotees of the Lord can answer such a question. They know that *Parama Puruśa* can make the lame walk and the mute speak. The Lord is not a passive onlooker to the universe, nor is He something imagined. Rather He is in everyone's heart, in every breath and pervading every thought. He is the lord of the devotee's heart, eternally shining in a blaze of glory.

### Eternal Shelter

Although Bábá had "Class One" status from the beginning of His incarceration, the rest of us in early 1972 had not yet been recognized as such. Every evening we were pushed inside our cells next to Bábá's at 6:00 but because we were allowed to serve Bábá His evening meal, our cell would be opened at 8:30 and we would join Bábá. At 9:00 two of us would stay with Bábá in His cell through the night.

He would stroll in the narrow pathway in front of the cell and we would join Him for this walk. The guards frequently were late in opening our door. Bábá would not wait, but would come straight to us, calling, "My little boys, what are you all doing?"

The guard had already opened our cell giving us strict orders to be back to the cell early. Now, other prisoners were not deprived in this regard, why single us out for this treatment? We did not like their biased behaviour. We would follow what was allowed in the jail regulations and return to the cell only a few minutes before nine. The guards were particularly displeased with us. But after all, why should we submit to their whim? So that evening we got into a heated argument with the guard.

Bábá came out of His cell just then. Hearing our voices, He demanded, "Barun, who is shouting over there?"

Barun Kumar replied, "Yes, Bábá. The guard is taunting us making brash threats."

Enough of a provocation! It was like pouring oil onto fire. Bábá flared up in a rage, and the whole atmosphere trembled with Bábá's fury. What a rebuke to the guard! The guard did not have the courage to face such an explosion, clearly frightened he looked for an escape. He retreated in silence. After that he never showed us any discourteous behaviour.

At nine we went into Bábá's cell. Bábá talked a little about the incident. "You have seen how a mother hen protects her little ones from the eagle's claws by putting them under her wings? I do the same with my disciples because they are entirely dependent on me. I make their path free from all

dangers and provide them with all-round protection. That is my dharma."

Moved by His sublime statement, we were all ears. Bábá started again, "That is why, you know, I get extremely annoyed if disciples leave my presence without my consent. Before they go, I want to see whether their return journeys will be safe, whether they will have any trouble or risk their lives on the way. If I see any such possibility I immediately ask them not to undertake the journey. In many cases, they say that they can't wait because their reservations have already been made for that particular day. Others say that they have urgent official work which cannot be deferred. Again some others say that they have no leave due to them, and so on. I don't pay heed to these excuses, and forbid them from leaving on that particular day. I find some way to make them stay on for a few days. I never disclose the real reason to them, however. Nor do I hesitate to violate the laws of nature or will of Providence, if I find it necessary to save someone from sure death. I must do that, for that, of course, is my way, my responsibility."

How true were these words! Many disciples have experienced some occasion when Bábá had forbidden them to leave on some pretext. Often they found out later something which convinced them of the reason for His delaying them. Sometimes Bábá would send instructions to some disciple in some far-flung corner of the world that would save him or her from some terrible consequences. Bábá would not just send out orders, he would see that those instructions were carried out and that the dangerous situation was passed safely.

Regarding danger from evil elements, Bábá once said in 1967, "If one does his or her *sádhaná* regularly twice a day and tries to follow conduct rules strictly, then no one will be able to do any harm to him or her. Rather, whoever entertains evil designs against any devotee will find the reaction boomeranging back against themselves. No matter how powerful they may think they are, they are sure to be annihilated."

Many *sádhakas* have experienced this. Whatever is based on dharma and truth will be victorious. *Yato dharmah tato Iśta, yato Iśtah tato jayah* – "Where there is dharma there is *Iśta*, and where there is *Iśta*, there is victory." Those who go against dharma ultimately disappear in dark oblivion.

Tantric literature gives a description of the Supreme Entity: "He is the most fearsome of the fearsome, the saviour of saviours, the vital force of all living beings and the purest of the pure." He is the guru of the universe, the most beloved jewel of the devotee's heart. We take shelter at His feet. To that end, we have to make our minds one-pointed. Bábá told us a story in this context.

There was a lovely pond where there were many fish. Small ones and big ones, they lived happily together. One day a few fishermen were walking by the side of the pond. They were planning to come the next morning and catch all the fish in the pond. A small fish overheard them talking. The news spread rapidly in the fish community, and an emergency meeting was called to discuss the matter. Many wise speeches were delivered by the leader-fishes. Each gave its opinion as to what to do.

The first fish spoke. His name was Thousand-Brain. He took the news lightly. He boasted, "I know thousands of strategies because I have such a lot of brains. If I am caught at all, I will cleverly jump out of the nets and escape."

The next speaker was Hundred-Brain, and with a flourish he said, "I know hundreds of tactics to evade the nets. I, too,

will jump out immediately. I won’t be trapped.”

Lastly came an ordinary fish. His name was One-Brain. He said, “I don’t possess a clever and sharp mind like all of you. When I hear of this impending danger, I think it will be wisest if I just move myself and my family to a safe place.” So One-Brain took his family to another pond nearby.

The next morning the fishermen came and spread their nets. They caught all the fish in the pond. The fishermen happily left with their big catch. They passed the other pond. One-Brain was there. He turned to his wife and said, “Dear, look there! That heavy one on top of the basket the fisherman is carrying is Hundred-Brain, and that one dangling in the other fisherman’s hand is Thousand-Brain, but I, One-Brain, remain behind swimming happily in the clear waters.”

“So you see,” Bábá said when he finished the story, “one-pointedness is absolutely necessary for the devotees. A devotee must withdraw his or her mind from everything else and surrender to the Supreme Saviour. Only He should be the object of the devotee’s thoughts, dreams and imagination. When the supreme Saviour is there, there is nothing to fear. The devotee should therefore be free from all despair and fear. For the Supreme alone is the final liberator of all.” He then quoted a verse:

> You alone do I remember, you alone do I worship.
> To You the eternal witness of the world I pay my salutations –
> You, the eternal Lord, the only shelter of all beings,
> The boat that crosses the ocean of this world.
> I take shelter in You, it is my only way out.

# CHAPTER 19

# ETERNAL “I”

There are three words in Bengali derived from the root verb *dish*. They are *upadesha*, *nirdesha* and *ádesha*. Possibly not many people know the difference of usage among these three similar words. Bábá in *Shabda Cayaniká*, a monumental Bengali encyclopaedia and lexicon, has given elaborate analysis of these words.

Where an elaborate definition is given about what should and should not be done, and advice is given in addition, we say it is *upadesha*, which translates as “advice”. Where, after explaining what should and should not be done, positive direction is given, it is *nirdesha*, that is, “pointing out” or “directions”. Where an order or command is made, whether explanations are given or not, it is *ádesha*, or “command”. I do not know if anybody else has made such a lucid analysis of these three. But the discourses that Bábá gave us regarding life and the world were – and are – invaluable. *Upadesha*, *nirdesha* or *ádesha* – the explanations were so thorough and deep that they made an indelible impression on my mind.

Bábá once said, “I have asked many people to observe many things. In some cases, I have explained the utility of such observances, taking into consideration the specific conditions of time and place, as well as the person concerned. Everyone has been made to appreciate their benefits. In some cases, however, thorough analysis has not been possible, due to lack of time, or I didn’t think it necessary to analyse. People have been asked simply to follow my instructions carefully. Whatever I say to anybody in any circumstance, it is always highly pertinent. Those who

follow my instructions are sure to benefit and attain success. But those who will try to evade them on some pretext or other, are sure to be deprived of the opportunity for success.”

It is not merely a question of advice, direction or order. Each of Bábá’s questions, statements and gestures was pregnant with meaning which only a discerning mind could interpret.

We could only partially realize the justification of some of these instructions at the time; the rest became clear only sometime afterwards, when we had nothing to do but shed tears in fond memory.

It was 1975 or 1976. Let us return to the memories of Bankipur Central Jail. We have been imprisoned for some years. The CBI was conducting the investigation in the case against us. Their motive was not merely to establish Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti as a common criminal; they were determined to cut the very root of the organization. The Emergency came to them as a boon. The entire country was alarmed at their violent high-handedness. Every citizen was seized with panic; more so the case of the Margis.

The Special Branch personnel enjoyed unlimited powers. They were immensely powerful and could destroy anyone they pleased. A bad report from them could lose anyone their job or send them to prison. Ananda Marga with all its branches was banned by the government. They constantly shadowed every movement of Ananda Marga. All Margis spent many an uneasy day under the threat of losing their jobs or even of assassination. In case of any problem, what would happen to their families? How would they carry on? Everyone was paralysed with fear.

If people are oppressed under an unjust machinery, they will be demoralized, they will lose their faith in dharma. Innocent people had come to Ananda Marga – the path of bliss – with pious devotion, and now faced tremendous threats to their lives and families. Many Margis were middle-class family men. Their families would be ruined if they lost their jobs or were imprisoned. Margis would be forced to denounce their affiliation with the mission. This was the hope of the then government – that Ananda Marga would just fold under these heavy threats to the personal security of the members.

Isolated in the Bankipur jail, we were terribly upset. Not that we were afraid for ourselves; we were absolutely sure of the eventual victory of dharma. We were in near-complete isolation from the outside world. Only occasionally did our lawyer or someone else come to see us, after running the gauntlet of red tape to secure the necessary permit from the authorities. We would wait eagerly for them. We had no other means of keeping in touch with the world outside. We had little to do inside the jail, and in the Emergency days, there was practically nothing to keep us engaged.

Bábá would lie on the bed with His eyes closed for long spans of time. It would be wrong to suggest that He was sleeping; where He was travelling, nobody knew. Only now and then would He open His eyes, and ask about some Margi, “Well, how is M— doing? Is he all right?”

“We don’t know, Bábá. We have no information here,” I would reply.

“Ah, yes.” Bábá would close His eyes again. In this way Bábá frequently asked about many Margis. Sometimes He seemed very anxious about someone. We never understood why, out of thousands of devotees, Bábá suddenly felt concerned about some particular person and wanted to know about his well-being.

We, on our part, would simply say, “We don’t have any

idea, Bábá. We guess they must be doing well.” Hearing our standard answer, Bábá would be silent again.

We could understand at least this much: that Bábá’s sudden concern for some Margi was not meaningless. So we never took it lightly, but waited anxiously for some visitor to come with news. When anyone came, we would thoroughly interrogate them regarding the Margis. We would convey to them Bábá’s occasional concerns about particular devotees. The different visitors would be astonished to hear this. It was strange. Invariably, the day and time of Bábá’s anxious inquiries coincided with some severe ordeal that this or that Margi was passing through at the time. Some of them were victims of the CBI’s vendetta against us, of threats to their jobs, or of humiliating harassment from their supervisors. At those moments they had been praying to the Lord for rescue.

And miracle after miracle! We heard story after story of how the dark clouds of danger, suffering and humiliation were removed from their lives by some unseen power. It was if some invisible Being was bending the forces of nature to His will, clearing away all obstacles in the paths of the devoted disciples of Ánandamúrtijii.

I came to realize that Bábá was not trying to get any practical information from us about the different Margis. Rather through these means we were made aware of that He was all-knowing and the saviour of the devotees.

It is worth mentioning here that about ten thousand Margis were put behind bars at one time or another at different places on various false charges. When the Emergency was over in 1977, Bábá asked me, “Can you tell me how many Margis lost their jobs as a result of this sacrifice for their ideology, having been victimized by political power-mongers?”

I had questioned many people and found that the answer was none. He continued, “Rather this imprisonment and suffering for ideology has increased their status and their respect among their friends and in the society.”

### Last Moments

It was shortly before October 21, 1990. I was then Rector Master of Anandanagar and had been terribly busy with its huge development programme day and night. I didn’t have time to come often to see Bábá. One day, however, I had to come to Calcutta for some important work. As luck would have it, an opportunity opened for me to have a private meeting with Bábá for some length of time. In the course of our conversation, the name of the respected Ácárya Amulya Ratan Sarangi came up. Bábá suddenly asked, “Well. Does Amulya sing my Prabhát Saḿgiita?”

“Yes, Bábá,” I said, “he is very fond of Prabhát Saḿgiita.”

“Can you tell me which songs he likes the most?”

I had heard Ác. Amulya Ratan frequently singing one particular song. It was a very passionate song, so I mentioned it to Bábá.

“Can you sing me the song?” Bábá asked.

Such a sudden request from Bábá made me somewhat puzzled. I was in a dilemma because I was never a good singer, nor did I remember any song completely. But surely due to Bábá’s grace, that whole song was in my mind. So, there was no question of my not singing, despite my lack of musical abilities, because it was Bábá’s desire that I sing a song to Him. I then sang the song, in my off-key tones, from beginning to end:

> Oh Lord, my dearest, it is You
> For whom my tears flow ceaselessly
> Far away have You gone

> Leaving me alone
> On the river-bank here.
>
> My love knows only You
> For Your delight is my song
> All my sentiments and passions
> Move around my Lord.
>
> Was it because I had no *sádhaná*
> Or not enough power of virtue or pious thoughts
> Is that why You left me,
> To go far away?
> You left never to return.

Bábá listened to the song with rapt attention. He seemed no longer there, His entire being transported elsewhere. He was completely unaware when the song had stopped. I was silently looking at Him, finding no words to express my feelings. Bábá was in no mood to hear me, in any case. I waited mute.

After some moments, Bábá awoke from His trance and asked, “Why? Is the song over, my son?”

My voice was almost inaudible, “Yes, Bábá, it is over.”

Then, suddenly He said, “Well, do you like poetry?”

“Yes, Bábá, I do,”

“Which one of Rabindranath’s poems do you like the most?”

I said, “‘Duh-Samaya’, ‘Varśa-sheśa’, ‘Udbodhana’ ‘Suprabhát’…”

“Why not ‘Cira-Ámi’?” Bábá asked. (*Cira-Ámi* means “the Eternal ‘I’”)

“I don’t know that one, Bábá.”

“You don’t know it? Well, listen. I will recite it for you.”

Bábá then started reciting the poem in His inimitable melodious voice. What an exquisite delivery! I cannot express it. I was simply absorbed. The poem is very meaningful. I will loosely translate a few lines:

> When my footprints will not be here
> On your journey’s path,
> I will not be here
> To ply my ferry boat,
> When my transactions will be closed,
> And my accounts will be settled,
> Nor will I visit this market anymore –
> You may not remember me then
> You not call me again
> Looking at the stars then.

***

> Who says I am not here
> In that morning?
> This “I” will be playing
> Through all these games.
> You will call me by new names,
> Bind me afresh in your arms,
> That Eternal “I” will come and go.

Finishing His recitation, Bábá became quiet for some moments. I, too, had lost my cue and did not know what to do or what to say.

Soon, Bábá came back to His normal self. Looking at me, He said, “Well, my little boy, you better go now, you have much work to do.”

I prostrated myself before Him and came out. For a long time the lines of the poem kept humming in my ears, and Bábá’s disinterested other-worldliness shone in my consciousness.

“Why did Bábá suddenly ask me to sing that particular

song?” I thought to myself. “And out of so many poems, why did He recite that particular Tagore poem? And with so much pathos! Is there some link between the two? To what distant world did He go – listening to me sing and Himself reciting that poem? Moreover, why, after all, did He ask me to go, saying in a completely detached voice ‘You have much work in your hands?’ All these mysterious questions made me totally confused.

Anyway, finishing my work in Calcutta, I returned to Anandanagar. A few days later came the news that Bábá had made His great journey, leaving His mortal frame behind. The exclusive and unique experiences of that day – Bábá asking me to sing a particular song, then His recitation of that particular poem, and lastly telling me to go for I have much work to do – all flashed through my mind.

I felt guilty. It was a pity I could not grasp the significance of His gestures that day. Why could I not hold Him back with us, being so close to Him that day? I cursed myself. For I could not offer my last worship with tears! My whole being was in turmoil at the loss of the greatest wealth of my life. That I would never again have the fortune of seeing His lotus feet was beyond my comprehension. Hearing that shocking news, I lost control of myself and fell down in utter despair, my whole consciousness engulfed in the dark clouds of helplessness. But within moments my consciousness was brightened by a new ray of light. I could hear the music of another song humming within me:

> As You were in the past,
> You will also be in the future
> You, the Lord everlasting
> Infinite is Your *liilá*, never-ending.
>
> –Prabhát Saḿgiita

The scene of Bábá reciting the last few lines of “Cira-Ámi” came back to mind:

> Who says I am not here
> In that morning?
> This “I” will be playing
> Through all these games.

Still today, His presence is there as before, though not in the outer world. It is in the world of the mind, in the heart of hearts that He is ever-present with us.

Occasionally Bábá would recite this devotional poem:

> I am always in Vrindavan
> I am not without my Hari
> The Lord will respond to my heartfelt call
> And come closer and nearer to me.

If you call Him earnestly, He is sure to respond. He will make His home in your heart, illumining your inner self. “This ‘I’ will be playing through all these games.” To know Him, rituals, and worship with incense, candles or decorations, are unnecessary. Only through meditation, inner worship and passionate love can you attract Him. He will then take His permanent place in the temple of your soul. A divine light will then shine in your heart and soul; and you will be able to transcend the limits of this finite self in divine ecstasy. You will then be identified with the *Cira-Ámi* – the Eternal “I”. Your small self will be lost in the Universal Self; the small stream will merge in the infinite ocean of existence.

# GLOSSARY

**ÁCÁRYA** or **ÁCÁRYÁ.** Spiritual teacher qualified to give initiation and teach all lessons of meditation.
**ADHARMA.** That which goes against DHARMA.
**ÁNANDA.** Divine bliss.
**ANANDA MARGA.** Path of divine bliss; Ananda Marga Pracáraka Saḿgha (Ananda Marga organization).
**ÁSANA.** The third limb of *aśtáḿga* (eight-limbed) *yoga*. *Ásanas*: postures for curing physical problems, especially those that interfere with SÁDHANÁ.
**ÁSHRAM.** A spiritual centre.
**AVADHÚTA** or **AVADHÚTIKÁ.** Literally, “one who is thoroughly cleansed mentally and spiritually”; a monk or nun of an order close to the tradition of Shaeva Tantra.

**BÁBÁ.** Affectionate name for Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti.
**BHAKTI.** Devotion.
**BRAHMA.** Supreme Entity comprising both *Puruśa*, or Supreme Consciousness, and *Prakrti*, or Operative Principle.

**DÁDÁ.** Elder brother.
**DIDI.** Elder sister.
**DHARMA.** Characteristic property; spirituality; the path of righteousness in social affairs. Adjective form: *dhármika*.
**DHARMA MAHÁCAKRA.** A special spiritual gathering addressed by MÁRGA GURU.
**DHYÁNA.** Meditation in which the psyche is directed towards Consciousness.

**EKÁDASHII.** “Eleventh” day after the new moon or full moon, days on which fasting is especially advantageous.

**GURUDEVA.** A respectful form of address for one’s own guru.

**IŚTA.** Goal; one’s personal deity or goal in life.

**JÁGRTI.** Literally, “place for awakening”; Ananda Marga spiritual centre.

**KARMA.** Action; sometimes, positive or negative action which produces SAḾSKÁRAs.

**KIIRTAN.** Collective singing of the name of the Lord, sometimes combined with a dance that expresses the spirit of surrender.

**MANTRA.** A sound or collection of sounds which, when meditated upon, will lead to spiritual liberation. A mantra is incantative, pulsative, and ideative.

**MÁRGA GURU.** Preceptor of Ananda Marga, Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti.

**MARGI.** A member of ANANDA MARGA.

**PARAMA PURUŚA.** Supreme Consciousness.

**PRABHÁT SAḾGIITA.** A body of 5018 spiritual and psycho-spiritual songs composed by Prabhat Ranjan Sarkar (Shrii Shrii Ánandamúrti).

**SADÁSHIVA.** Shiva (literally, “Eternal Shiva”).

**SÁDHAKA.** Spiritual practitioner.

**SÁDHANÁ.** Literally, “sustained effort”; spiritual practice; meditation.

**SÁDHU.** In common usage in India for monks or religious mendicants.

**SAḾSKÁRA.** Mental reactive momentum, potential mental reaction.

**SANNYÁSII** or **SANNYÁSINII.** Literally, “one who has surrendered one’s everything to the Cosmic will” or “one who ensconces oneself in *Sat*, the unchangeable entity”; a renunciant.

**TANTRA.** A spiritual tradition which originated in India in prehistoric times and was first systematized by Shiva. It emphasizes the development of human vigour, both through meditation and through confrontation of difficult external situations, to overcome all fears and weaknesses. Also, a scripture expounding that tradition.

**YAMA AND NIYAMA.** Moral code.
